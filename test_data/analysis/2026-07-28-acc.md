# Paper Analysis: arXiv:2607.24672

**Title:** Explainable Reinforcement Learning via Physics-Aware Policy Distillation  
**Authors:** Shaker Al-Tamari, Waled Kadour  
**Overall Score:** **Reject**

## 1. Problem Statement

The paper studies whether a black-box continuous-control reinforcement learning policy can be replaced by an interpretable decision tree while retaining closed-loop control performance. It uses `InvertedPendulum-v4`, trains a TD3 teacher, and distills its policy into a depth-7 decision tree augmented with a hand-designed feature, “Pole Urgency,” combining pole angle and angular velocity.

The motivation is sound: safety-critical systems need policy representations that can be inspected, audited, and potentially verified. The paper further argues that evaluating interpretability alone is inadequate; one must also evaluate the dynamic consequences of replacing continuous neural actions with piecewise-constant tree actions.

## 2. Main Arguments

### Argument 1: A shallow decision tree can match the TD3 teacher's control performance

- **Claim:** A depth-7 decision-tree surrogate reaches the teacher's performance, with a 100% success rate over 50 episodes and return 1000.
- **Supporting evidence:** The paper reports a perfect teacher return and a 100% student success rate over 50 evaluation episodes.
- **Assumptions:** The evaluation distribution, initialization conditions, disturbances, random seeds, and episode sample are representative of meaningful closed-loop behavior.

The evidence is insufficient. No numerical table, confidence interval, per-seed result, failure count under perturbation, or evaluation protocol is provided. On a simple deterministic benchmark, 50 nominal episodes do not establish equivalence to a teacher policy.

### Argument 2: Physics-aware feature engineering permits a shallow, interpretable tree

- **Claim:** The feature \(P_{\text{urgency}} = \theta + K_d \dot{\theta}\) lets an axis-aligned tree approximate diagonal control boundaries, thereby preserving performance with limited depth.
- **Supporting evidence:** The paper presents a conceptual decision-boundary visualization and asserts that the feature produces diagonal switching surfaces.
- **Assumptions:** The selected derivative gain \(K_d\) is appropriate, the engineered feature materially improves distillation, and the resulting rules remain understandable to target auditors.

This is plausible but unsubstantiated. The paper provides neither the value nor selection method for \(K_d\), and it has no ablation comparing: raw-state trees, trees with Pole Urgency, deeper trees, oblique trees, linear/PD controllers, or other interpretable policy classes.

### Argument 3: Noisy Oracle Rollouts improve recovery behavior and robustness

- **Claim:** Adding Gaussian action noise while collecting teacher rollouts exposes the dataset to near-failure states and teaches recovery behavior.
- **Supporting evidence:** The argument is stated conceptually; no robustness or distributional results are reported.
- **Assumptions:** The noise sufficiently and safely explores relevant recovery states, and the labels assigned to disturbed states reflect the unperturbed expert's desired corrective action.

The data-generation procedure is ambiguous. It is not specified whether the supervised label is the noisy applied teacher action or a fresh deterministic teacher query at the perturbed state. These alternatives have very different implications: cloning injected noise would degrade the student, whereas querying the teacher at the induced state would more closely resemble DAgger-style policy extraction.

### Argument 4: Quantized decision-tree control creates Bang-Bang behavior but remains BIBO-stable

- **Claim:** Tree quantization induces high-frequency switching and a bimodal limit cycle, yet preserves BIBO stability.
- **Supporting evidence:** The paper refers to angle-distribution and control-effort plots.
- **Assumptions:** Observed finite-horizon trajectories establish BIBO stability, the angular bounds are correctly reported, and the chattering measure is quantitatively defined.

This central claim is technically unsupported and internally inconsistent. The paper states that success requires \(|\theta| < 0.2095\) radians, yet claims the stable bimodal cycle occurs at approximately \(\pm 0.5\) radians. A trajectory oscillating around \(\pm 0.5\) radians cannot satisfy the stated failure constraint or survive for 1,000 steps. This contradiction invalidates the paper's headline stability interpretation until resolved.

## 3. Methodology

The teacher is a TD3 actor with two 256-unit ReLU hidden layers and a tanh output, trained using CleanRL. Reported hyperparameters include learning rate \(3 \times 10^{-4}\), batch size 256, and Gaussian exploration noise with standard deviation 0.1.

For distillation, rollouts from the teacher are collected, reportedly emphasizing early transients and states induced by noisy actions. A scikit-learn decision-tree regressor with maximum depth 7 predicts actions from the physical state augmented with Pole Urgency. The paper evaluates task success, return, action MSE and \(R^2\), tree complexity, maximum angle deviation, and action switching/chattering.

There are no actual theoretical derivations establishing stability. The stated conclusion is empirical: the tree allegedly remains bounded despite a discrete limit cycle. BIBO stability is not demonstrated by a system model, an input class, a bound on quantization error, a Lyapunov argument, a reachability analysis, or a closed-loop proof.

## 4. Review

### Strengths

- The paper addresses a real and important issue: interpretable control policies for safety-sensitive RL.
- It correctly emphasizes that surrogate fidelity alone is insufficient; closed-loop behavior matters.
- The use of an engineered state feature to align a simple model with control dynamics is intuitive and potentially useful.
- The paper identifies action chattering as an important deployment concern often omitted from policy-distillation evaluations.
- The distinction between global rule visualization and local decision-path explanation is clear at a conceptual level.

### Weaknesses

- **The principal stability result is contradictory.** Pages 2 and 5 respectively specify a failure threshold of \(\pm 0.2095\) radians and a purported safe limit cycle near \(\pm 0.5\) radians. These claims cannot both be true in the described environment. The reported result, figure caption, units, threshold, or environment configuration must be incorrect.

- **BIBO stability is claimed without a stability analysis.** An observed bounded trajectory over 50 episodes is not a proof of BIBO stability. In particular, the paper never defines the admissible disturbances, input norm, output norm, controller switching behavior, or closed-loop dynamics required by the claim. “Mathematically proves” is therefore materially misleading.

- **The paper does not report the quantitative results it claims to evaluate.** MSE, \(R^2\), leaf count, switching frequency, maximum deviation, action magnitudes, and reward dispersion are introduced as metrics but never reported numerically. Plots without scales, tabulated values, variability, and experimental protocol do not support the conclusions.

- **There is no ablation establishing the contribution of the proposed method.** The central novelty is the combination of Pole Urgency and Noisy Oracle Rollouts, but neither component is isolated. The reader cannot determine whether success is due to the feature, tree depth, a favorable teacher, the easy benchmark, rollout noise, or basic behavioral cloning.

- **The connection to VIPER is inaccurate or incomplete.** The paper presents the VIPER objective and student-induced state-distribution motivation, but does not implement or describe iterative dataset aggregation, teacher Q-value weighting, teacher critic usage for weighting, or the selection of a policy across iterations. The actual method appears to be one-pass supervised regression on teacher rollouts, not VIPER.

- **The noisy-rollout procedure is underspecified.** Dataset size, rollout count, action-noise scale, seed count, state distribution, labeling process, reset conditions, and near-failure coverage are absent. The claim that the dataset captures emergency recovery logic is consequently not reproducible.

- **The empirical setting is too weak for the stated safety and real-world claims.** `InvertedPendulum-v4` is a low-dimensional, short-horizon simulation benchmark with a survival reward. Perfect nominal reward is not evidence for autonomous-driving, robotics, certification, regulatory compliance, or safety-layer viability.

- **Interpretability is asserted rather than evaluated.** A depth bound and a visualized tree do not establish that the policy is comprehensible, auditable, or suitable for safety review. The “global” boundary map additionally fixes cart position and velocity, so it visualizes only a two-dimensional slice of a higher-dimensional policy, not the global policy.

- **The decision-tree output is not necessarily Bang-Bang control.** A regression tree produces piecewise-constant actions with potentially many leaf values; that differs from a binary Bang-Bang controller. The paper should measure action-level cardinality, switching thresholds, dwell times, spectral content, and actuator-relevant switching rates before using this control-theoretic characterization.

- **Reproducibility is inadequate.** Important TD3 details are omitted, including training steps, replay-buffer size, discount factor, target-network update coefficient, policy delay, target policy noise, clipping, action scaling, normalization, deterministic evaluation procedure, hardware details, package versions, random seeds, and model-selection criteria.

- **The citation mapping contains serious errors.** The text attributes Bastani et al. to reference [8], but [8] is Tabrez and Hayes; the actual Bastani paper appears as [12]. It similarly cites Tabrez and Hayes as [6], while [6] is ISO 26262. These errors weaken confidence in the literature review and technical positioning.

### Recommendations

- Correct the angle-limit contradiction before any further interpretation of the stability results. Include raw trajectories, axis units, environment termination settings, and code/configuration sufficient to reproduce the figure.
- Replace the BIBO-stability claim with a careful empirical boundedness claim unless a proper closed-loop analysis is supplied. A stronger version should provide a quantization-error bound tied to system dynamics, or a Lyapunov, invariant-set, reachability, or robust-control analysis.
- Report all metrics in numerical tables with mean, standard deviation or confidence intervals, per-seed results, and the exact number of successful and failed episodes.
- Add controlled ablations for Pole Urgency, noisy rollout collection, tree depth, dataset size, action-noise magnitude, and state-feature choices.
- Compare against meaningful baselines: plain behavioral cloning, iterative DAgger/VIPER-style extraction, classical PD/LQR control, a raw-state decision tree, an oblique tree, and an interpretable policy trained directly by RL such as DTPO.
- Precisely define Noisy Oracle Rollouts, especially whether teacher labels are queried after the disturbance and whether noise is applied to actions, states, observations, or all three.
- Evaluate robustness under changed initial states, external forces, observation noise, parameter variation, and actuator noise. Report distribution shift explicitly.
- Quantify chattering with switching rate, action total variation, dwell-time distribution, and a control-effort or mechanical-cost metric. Evaluate any smoothing method in closed loop, since low-pass filtering can destabilize delayed feedback systems.
- Temper claims regarding certification, ISO compliance, autonomous driving, and physical deployment. Explainability does not itself provide formal verification or compliance.
- Correct all in-text citation mappings and distinguish clearly between background on VIPER and the algorithm actually implemented.

## Paper Metadata

- **Title:** Evaluating Fuzz Testing for Reinforcement Learning Agents
- **Authors:** Zhibin Kang, Hanmo You, Dong Wang, Haiming Zheng, Junjie Chen
- **Venue/style:** Journal of LaTeX Class Files formatting; the manuscript identifies itself as arXiv:2607.24577v1
- **Research area:** Reinforcement learning testing, fuzz testing, empirical software engineering
- **Studied environments:** MountainCar, BipedalWalker, and CARLA
- **Compared methods:** Random Testing, MDPFuzz, CureFuzz, Generative Model-Based Testing, SeqDivFuzz, and QD-based Policy Testing

## 1. Problem Statement

The paper studies how existing fuzz-testing methods for reinforcement-learning agents compare when evaluated under a common experimental protocol.

The motivation is that RL agents are increasingly used in safety-sensitive domains, but their failures may occur only under rare or difficult environmental configurations. Existing RL-fuzzing papers generally report results under different environments, agent implementations, parameter settings, metrics, and computational budgets. As a result, it is difficult to determine:

- which fuzzers actually discover more failures;
- whether they discover different failure behaviors;
- how much computation they require;
- whether the discovered failures are useful for downstream safety tasks.

The paper therefore proposes a unified empirical comparison along four dimensions:

1. crash-detection effectiveness;
2. crash diversity;
3. testing efficiency;
4. practical utility for agent repair and safety monitoring.

This is an important and relevant problem. However, the paper’s conclusions depend heavily on whether the comparison protocol is genuinely fair, whether “crash” and “unique crash” are operationalized rigorously, and whether the downstream experiments avoid data leakage and class-imbalance artifacts.

## 2. Main Arguments

### Argument 1: MDPFuzz is generally the strongest method for crash discovery

**Claim.** Throughput-oriented methods, particularly the updated MDPFuzz implementation, outperform competing fuzzers in crash discovery.

**Supporting evidence reported in the manuscript.**

- The abstract states that MDPFuzz demonstrates superior effectiveness and efficiency.
- The RQ1 summary claims that MDPFuzz consistently outperforms other methods across the three benchmarks.
- The authors argue that high testing throughput is especially useful for discovering crashes.

**Assumptions.**

- The updated MDPFuzz implementation is faithful to the intended method and comparable to the implementations of other methods.
- The methods receive equivalent testing budgets.
- The definition of a valid and unique crash does not favor MDPFuzz’s generation mechanism.
- The evaluated environments and agents are representative enough to support the general claim.

**Assessment.**

This could be a useful empirical finding, but the claim is currently too broad. “Consistently outperforming” should be supported with per-environment, per-budget curves and uncertainty estimates. It is also important to distinguish the original MDPFuzz algorithm from the “updated version” adopted from later replication work. Otherwise, the result may primarily compare a revised heuristic implementation against older or less optimized implementations of competing methods.

### Argument 2: SeqDivFuzz discovers more diverse crash behaviors

**Claim.** Methods explicitly designed to promote exploration, especially SeqDivFuzz, uncover more diverse crashes than efficiency-oriented methods.

**Supporting evidence reported in the manuscript.**

- The abstract identifies SeqDivFuzz as strongest for diverse crash behaviors.
- The RQ2 summary reports a trade-off between crash quantity and crash diversity.
- SeqDivFuzz uses a sequence-diversity inference module and early terminates redundant test cases.

**Assumptions.**

- The sequence-diversity metric is a meaningful proxy for semantically distinct failures.
- The Siamese-network evaluator generalizes across all three environments.
- More diverse trajectories correspond to more useful or consequential failure modes.
- The method is not advantaged by the paper’s particular crash deduplication procedure.

**Assessment.**

The diversity result is plausible, but “diversity” is not automatically equivalent to “distinct failure cause,” “severity,” or “safety relevance.” A trajectory-level embedding may count small variations of the same failure as different crashes. Conversely, two qualitatively different failures may be considered similar if the representation misses the relevant distinction. The paper should report multiple diversity levels, such as input-space diversity, trajectory-state diversity, action diversity, failure-location diversity, and semantic or manually validated failure categories.

### Argument 3: Mutation-based fuzzers are more efficient than generative methods

**Claim.** Mutation-based methods require fewer iterations and less resource expenditure per unique crash than generative methods.

**Supporting evidence reported in the manuscript.**

- The RQ3 summary states that mutation-based methods substantially outperform generative methods in efficiency.
- The paper emphasizes testing throughput and the computational overhead of learned generation models.

**Assumptions.**

- “Iteration,” wall-clock time, environment steps, CPU/GPU utilization, and memory are measured consistently.
- Model-training and preprocessing costs are included for generative methods.
- The comparison uses equivalent hardware and parallelism.
- The number of unique crashes is a stable denominator.

**Assessment.**

This comparison is only meaningful if the full lifecycle cost of each method is measured. Generative methods often incur offline training, data collection, model fitting, and inference costs, whereas mutation methods may incur more environment interactions. The paper should report both:

- environment-interaction efficiency, such as crashes per million environment steps; and
- end-to-end wall-clock efficiency, including initialization, model training, inference, and validity checking.

Otherwise, the result could reflect an accounting choice rather than an inherent advantage of mutation-based fuzzing.

### Argument 4: Fuzzing-generated crashes improve agent robustness

**Claim.** Crashes found by fuzzing can be used to repair or retrain RL agents, and diversity-oriented methods produce the largest robustness improvements.

**Supporting evidence reported in the manuscript.**

- The abstract reports meaningful robustness improvements from fuzzing-generated crashes.
- The RQ4 summary states that most methods improve robustness, with diversity-oriented methods providing the largest gains.
- A fixed set of 1,000 random configurations is retained as an independent test set.

**Assumptions.**

- The repair procedure is equivalent across methods.
- The crashes used for retraining are representative rather than merely easy-to-memorize examples.
- Improvements on the random test set reflect real robustness rather than distribution-specific adaptation.
- Retraining does not substantially alter nominal-task performance.

**Assessment.**

This is potentially the most practically valuable part of the paper, but it requires especially careful controls. The manuscript should compare fuzzing-based repair against:

- random-data augmentation;
- ordinary failure replay;
- adversarial training;
- increased training duration without additional crash data;
- a matched number of training examples sampled from each method.

It should also measure regression on nominal scenarios, performance on held-out fuzzers, and robustness against unseen environment perturbations. A single random test set is insufficient to establish broad robustness, especially if the fuzzers and test set share the same configuration distribution.

### Argument 5: Safety monitors trained on crash-triggering inputs generalize across fuzzing methods

**Claim.** A monitor trained on crash-triggering inputs can identify risky episodes with greater than 95% accuracy, including in cross-method evaluation.

**Supporting evidence reported in the manuscript.**

- The abstract reports strong cross-method generalization.
- The RQ4 discussion claims accuracy typically exceeds 95%.
- The authors interpret this as evidence that fuzzing exposes shared crash characteristics.

**Assumptions.**

- The data split is performed by scenario or environment configuration rather than by individual trajectory.
- Positive and negative classes are balanced or evaluated with suitable metrics.
- Accuracy is not dominated by the majority class.
- Cross-method test cases are genuinely independent and not near-duplicates of training cases.
- The monitor does not use features that leak the crash label.

**Assessment.**

Accuracy alone is not persuasive for a safety-monitoring claim. If crashes are rare, a trivial always-safe or always-non-crash classifier may obtain high accuracy. The paper should report precision, recall, F1, AUROC, AUPRC, false-negative rate, false-alarm rate, calibration, and detection lead time. For a safety monitor, the false-negative rate and warning horizon are more important than aggregate accuracy.

The cross-method result is also vulnerable to leakage if the same initial seeds, nearby mutations, or simulator states appear in both training and test data. The authors should explicitly use scenario-level and seed-family-level splits.

## 3. Methodology

### Overall approach

The study evaluates six strategies under a unified framework:

- Random Testing;
- MDPFuzz;
- CureFuzz;
- Generative Model-Based Testing, referred to as G-Model;
- SeqDivFuzz;
- QD-based Policy Testing, referred to as QDFuzz.

The methods are evaluated on three increasingly complex tasks:

| Environment | RL agent | Input dimensionality | Reported failure rate | Crash criterion |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| MountainCar | DQN | 2 dimensions | 1.1% | Car does not exceed position 0.5 within 200 steps |
| BipedalWalker | Truncated Quantile Critics | 15 dimensions | 1.6% | Robot falls within 300 steps |
| CARLA | Roach/PPO-based agent | 66 dimensions, 63 mutated | 7.2% | Collision or failure to reach destination within 200 steps |

The agents are selected to be relatively robust under random testing. The authors first sample 1,000 random configurations and use failure rates to select agents whose intrinsic failure rate is low enough that differences among fuzzers remain visible.

### Validity checking

The paper applies differential testing. A generated configuration is counted as a valid crash only if:

1. the target agent fails; and
2. an independently trained validation policy succeeds.

The validation policies are:

- PPO for MountainCar;
- SAC for BipedalWalker;
- CaRL for CARLA.

The reported validation-policy failure rates are 0%, 0%, and 2.5%, respectively.

This is a reasonable attempt to distinguish agent failures from intrinsically impossible scenarios. Nevertheless, it introduces a potentially substantial selection bias: a test case is discarded whenever the validation policy fails, even if the scenario is feasible for a competent policy but difficult for that particular validator. This issue is particularly serious in CARLA, where the validator itself has a reported 2.5% failure rate.

A stronger design would use multiple independent validation policies, simulator feasibility checks, expert demonstrations, or a graded validity criterion. At minimum, the paper should report how many candidate crashes are removed by validity checking for each method and environment.

### Crash uniqueness

The paper defines a unique crash jointly by:

- the initial seed or initial state; and
- the resulting crash trajectory or behavior.

This is an important detail, but it raises several concerns:

- In continuous input spaces, almost every mutation may have a different numerical initial state.
- Without discretization, clustering, or distance thresholds, “unique” can mean merely numerically different.
- Trajectory uniqueness requires a clear representation and threshold.
- Counting both seed and trajectory differences may double-count the same failure mechanism.
- The metric may favor methods that generate a wider range of initial configurations rather than methods that expose distinct defects.

The paper should specify the exact deduplication algorithm, feature representation, normalization, thresholds, sensitivity analysis, and reproducibility procedure.

### Study dimensions

The paper evaluates:

- effectiveness through the number of valid unique crashes;
- diversity through crash or trajectory diversity;
- efficiency through time and resource consumption;
- practical utility through robustness improvement and safety monitoring.

The comparison is intended to use unified configurations and corrected or updated implementations. The authors also state that code, datasets, and experimental artifacts will be released.

## 4. Overall Review

### Overall Score: Major Revision

The paper addresses a timely and important empirical question and has the potential to become a useful reference for RL-fuzzing research. The inclusion of random testing, multiple environments, downstream repair, and safety-monitoring experiments is valuable.

However, based on the available manuscript content, several central conclusions are not yet sufficiently supported. The most important concerns involve the fairness of comparing heterogeneous implementations, the validity of the “unique crash” definition, the bias introduced by a single validation policy, incomplete specification of computational budgets, and insufficiently rigorous evaluation of safety-monitoring performance.

The paper should not be accepted in its current form without substantial clarification and additional analysis.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important gap: existing RL-fuzzing studies are difficult to compare because of inconsistent protocols.
- Evaluates multiple methods under a stated unified framework.
- Includes random testing, which is an essential and often omitted baseline.
- Covers environments with substantially different complexity and dimensionality.
- Explicitly considers practical downstream uses rather than only counting discovered failures.
- Attempts to control for invalid scenarios using independently trained validation policies.
- Recognizes the trade-off between crash quantity, diversity, and efficiency.
- The proposed practical recommendation—combining complementary fuzzing strategies—is plausible and potentially useful.
- The planned release of code and artifacts could improve reproducibility and future benchmarking.

### Weaknesses and Specific Concerns

#### 1. The comparison may not be implementation-fair

The methods appear to originate from different papers, with different maturity levels, code quality, preprocessing pipelines, and computational requirements. The manuscript states that MDPFuzz uses an updated version, while the paper does not yet provide enough information to establish that all other methods receive comparable reimplementation effort and hyperparameter tuning.

The authors should report:

- exact code versions and commit identifiers;
- whether official code or reimplementations are used;
- modifications made to each method;
- hyperparameter-search budgets;
- default versus tuned parameters;
- whether authors of the original methods were consulted;
- hardware, parallelism, and simulator settings.

Without this information, the observed ranking may reflect implementation quality rather than algorithmic merit.

#### 2. “Unique crash” is underdefined for continuous state spaces

The proposed uniqueness criterion combines initial input and trajectory behavior, but this is problematic in MountainCar, BipedalWalker, and CARLA, where inputs are continuous or highly structured.

Two inputs that differ by an arbitrarily small amount may be counted as separate crashes. Conversely, distinct causal failures may be merged if their state trajectories are close under the selected representation. The paper needs an explicit and reproducible clustering procedure, including thresholds and a sensitivity analysis.

The authors should also report results under multiple definitions:

- exact or discretized input uniqueness;
- trajectory clustering;
- failure-location uniqueness;
- semantic failure categories;
- environment-level causal outcomes.

#### 3. Differential validity testing can discard genuine failures

Requiring a validation policy to succeed is conservative, but it does not prove that the generated scenario is feasible. It only proves that one particular policy succeeded. A robust but imperfect validator may fail in feasible scenarios, especially in CARLA.

This procedure could disproportionately remove the crashes generated by methods that explore more unusual regions of the input space. As a result, the evaluation may favor methods whose test cases remain close to the validator’s competence distribution.

The paper should provide:

- the fraction of generated cases rejected by validity checking;
- rejection rates broken down by method and environment;
- results with multiple validators;
- results without validity filtering;
- an analysis of whether rejected cases are concentrated in particular fuzzers.

#### 4. The crash oracle is not uniform across environments

The crash definitions differ substantially:

- MountainCar treats failure to reach a position threshold as a crash.
- BipedalWalker treats falling as a crash.
- CARLA combines collision and failure to reach the destination.

These are not necessarily comparable notions of safety failure. In CARLA, “failure to reach the destination within 200 steps” may reflect timeout, route difficulty, poor planning, or simulator dynamics rather than a crash in the conventional sense. Combining collision and timeout into one class may obscure important differences in severity and mechanism.

The study should report separate results for:

- collision;
- timeout;
- route failure;
- recoverable versus unrecoverable incidents;
- severity-weighted failures.

#### 5. Benchmark coverage is still limited

Three environments provide useful variation, but they do not establish broad conclusions about RL fuzzing. The selected benchmarks are mostly single-agent tasks with fixed agents and fixed environment families. They do not cover:

- multi-agent interaction;
- partial observability;
- nonstationary environments;
- stochastic safety-critical systems;
- discrete and hybrid action spaces in a balanced way;
- sim-to-real transfer;
- different RL algorithms under the same environment;
- different policies trained with different seeds.

The paper should moderate claims about RL fuzzing in general or expand the benchmark suite.

#### 6. The agent-selection protocol may induce selection bias

The authors intentionally choose robust agents so that fuzzer differences are visible. This is understandable, but it may make the benchmark less representative of practical testing, where agents can have heterogeneous failure profiles.

Selecting agents based on a 1,000-case random test also introduces sampling noise and possible benchmark overfitting. The study should report all candidate agents, the selection rule, confidence intervals for estimated failure rates, and sensitivity to the selected agent.

It would also be useful to evaluate both robust and non-robust agents. A method that is effective only when the base agent is already robust may not be generally useful.

#### 7. Efficiency claims require a clearer accounting model

The manuscript reports that mutation-based methods are more efficient, but the available text does not define the exact denominator or cost model. “Iterations to trigger each unique crash” can be misleading because methods may differ in:

- environment steps per iteration;
- early termination behavior;
- initialization cost;
- offline training;
- GPU utilization;
- parallel workers;
- validity-checking overhead;
- trajectory length.

Efficiency should be reported using several normalized measures, including crashes per environment step, crashes per wall-clock hour, CPU/GPU energy or resource consumption, and end-to-end cost. Confidence intervals and scaling curves are necessary.

#### 8. The safety-monitoring evaluation may be misleading if based on accuracy

The reported accuracy above 95% is insufficient to establish a reliable safety monitor. If crash episodes are rare, high accuracy can be achieved by predicting “no crash” almost always.

The paper should report:

- class balance;
- confusion matrices;
- precision and recall;
- false-negative rate;
- false-positive rate;
- AUROC and AUPRC;
- calibration;
- warning lead time;
- performance under distribution shift;
- performance at operationally meaningful false-alarm rates.

The cross-method generalization claim also requires strict scenario-level splits. Randomly splitting trajectories can place near-identical mutations of the same seed in both training and test sets.

#### 9. Robustness repair may confound data augmentation with algorithmic quality

If each fuzzer supplies a different number or distribution of crashes, the resulting repair performance may simply reflect the amount and difficulty of training data. To compare methods fairly, the authors should match:

- number of crash examples;
- number of environment transitions;
- number of unique seeds;
- training steps;
- replay-buffer composition.

Ablations should separate the effects of crash count, crash diversity, crash severity, and fuzzer identity. Nominal performance and regression faults should be measured in addition to robustness.

#### 10. Statistical reporting is essential for a comparative empirical paper

The claims use strong language such as “consistently outperforming,” “substantially higher efficiency,” and “strong cross-method generalization.” Such claims require repeated runs, confidence intervals, effect sizes, and appropriate statistical tests.

At least the following should be reported:

- multiple independent random seeds;
- per-run rather than only aggregate values;
- median and dispersion for crash counts;
- bootstrap confidence intervals;
- significance tests corrected for multiple comparisons;
- performance-over-time curves;
- area under the crash-discovery curve.

Single-run rankings are not reliable for stochastic fuzzers and stochastic RL environments.

#### 11. The “first comprehensive study” claim should be carefully substantiated

The paper repeatedly describes itself as the first comprehensive empirical study. This is a high-priority novelty claim and should be supported by a systematic literature search and a comparison table showing prior empirical studies, methods, environments, metrics, and downstream tasks.

The related-work section lists numerous methods, but the available text does not establish that no prior benchmark or replication study already covers a comparable scope.

#### 12. Reproducibility claims need concrete evidence

The paper states that code, datasets, and artifacts are publicly released. The final version should provide:

- a persistent repository or archive;
- exact environment versions;
- simulator and dependency versions;
- pretrained agents;
- generated seeds;
- configuration files;
- scripts for reproducing every table and figure;
- expected runtime and hardware requirements;
- documentation for CARLA setup.

CARLA experiments are particularly difficult to reproduce because simulator versions, maps, random seeds, traffic behavior, and rendering settings can materially affect results.

## 5. Recommendations

The following revisions would substantially strengthen the paper:

1. **Fully specify the evaluation protocol.** Define the testing budget in environment steps, wall-clock time, number of episodes, and parallel workers.

2. **Report exact implementation provenance.** Include repositories, commits, modifications, hyperparameters, and tuning procedures for all six methods.

3. **Rework crash deduplication.** Provide a formal clustering method for continuous inputs and trajectories, and report sensitivity to thresholds and representations.

4. **Quantify validity-filtering bias.** Report rejected cases by method and environment, and repeat the analysis with multiple validators and alternative feasibility checks.

5. **Disaggregate CARLA failure types.** Separate collisions, timeouts, route failures, and other terminal outcomes.

6. **Use repeated trials and uncertainty estimates.** Include at least several independent runs per method, with confidence intervals and statistical tests.

7. **Normalize efficiency carefully.** Report environment steps per unique crash, wall-clock time per unique crash, initialization cost, model-training cost, and hardware utilization.

8. **Strengthen diversity analysis.** Use multiple levels of diversity, including input, trajectory, failure location, action sequence, and semantic failure mode. Human or expert validation of clusters would be valuable.

9. **Add strong repair baselines.** Compare fuzzing-based repair with random augmentation, failure replay, adversarial training, and matched-size training sets.

10. **Make safety-monitoring evaluation safety-oriented.** Report false negatives, false alarms, PR curves, calibration, and lead time rather than relying primarily on accuracy.

11. **Prevent leakage in cross-method experiments.** Split by initial seed, mutation family, scenario, route, and possibly simulator randomization—not merely by trajectory.

12. **Evaluate generalization beyond the selected agents.** Test multiple training seeds, policies, RL algorithms, or at least multiple checkpoints per environment.

13. **Moderate broad conclusions.** The current evidence, even if confirmed by the missing sections, would support conclusions about the selected single-agent benchmarks and implementations, not necessarily RL fuzzing as a whole.

## Final Recommendation

**Major Revision**

The paper has a strong empirical-study premise and addresses a genuine need for standardized RL-fuzzing evaluation. Its inclusion of downstream robustness repair and safety monitoring is particularly promising. However, the central empirical conclusions require stronger methodological controls before they can be considered reliable. The most urgent issues are the definition of unique crashes, validator-induced selection bias, implementation fairness, computational-budget normalization, leakage-resistant downstream evaluation, and statistically rigorous reporting.

# Review of “What do Reward Models Memorize?”

## Paper Metadata

- Title: What do Reward Models Memorize?
- Authors: Ivo Verhoeven, Pushkar Mishra, Ekaterina Shutova
- Affiliations: ILLC, University of Amsterdam; Google DeepMind
- Paper ID: 2607.24484
- Version: arXiv v1
- Category: Machine Learning / Reward Modeling / RLHF / Interpretability

## 1. Problem Statement

### Problem

The paper studies what discriminatively trained reward models (RMs) memorize when trained on human preference data. Rather than treating memorization only as literal reproduction of training examples, the paper operationalizes memorization through counterfactual memorization: the difference between a model’s probability of correctly classifying a preference pair when the pair is included in training and when it is held out.

The central question is:

> Which properties of preference pairs make them easy for an RM to generalize, and which properties cause the RM to rely on memorization?

The paper analyzes this question using two multi-turn human preference datasets, PRISM and COMMUNITY.

### Motivation

Reward models are intended to approximate a latent human utility or preference function. If an RM instead exploits dataset-specific correlations, then optimizing against it can produce reward hacking or reward overoptimization. The paper connects this concern to known undesirable behaviors in aligned language models, including:

- Excessive verbosity
- Sycophancy
- Generic or vague responses
- Excessive formatting and stylization
- Dependence on model identity
- Socially undesirable or biased correlations

The motivation is important: identifying what RMs memorize could help explain why RLHF systems exhibit these behaviors and could inform better data curation, model training, and evaluation.

## 2. Main Arguments

### Argument 1: RMs disproportionately memorize easy, high-margin preference pairs

#### Claim

RMs exhibit high training memorization on preference pairs with large human preference margins, while their performance on unseen pairs with similar characteristics is substantially weaker. The authors interpret this as a misallocation of memorization toward easy examples.

#### Supporting evidence

The paper reports that:

- User preference margin is a dominant predictor of training memorization in both PRISM and COMMUNITY.
- The relationship between preference margin and training memorization is strongly positive.
- The association with testing generalization is substantially weaker.
- Regression models predict training memorization with \(R^2 = 0.56\) on PRISM and \(R^2 = 0.63\) on COMMUNITY.
- Preference pairs with high counterfactual memorization are therefore associated with large preference margins.

#### Assumptions

This interpretation assumes that:

- The observed difference between training and testing behavior is caused by memorization rather than differences in train/test composition.
- The preference margin is not merely a proxy for annotation noise, prompt difficulty, or user-specific sampling effects.
- The RM training procedure allows meaningful comparison of examples with different preference margins.
- A high margin should translate into robust generalization rather than merely easier interpolation.

A major concern is that the paper states that the preference margin is included in the PRISM RM training objective. If so, the PRISM result is at least partly induced by the training loss itself and is not an independent discovery of memorization behavior.

### Argument 2: RMs memorize dataset-specific shortcuts such as model identity and user recruitment artifacts

#### Claim

RMs learn associations between preference labels and features that are specific to the construction of the preference dataset, including:

- The identity of the response-generating model
- User-level metadata
- Recruitment-wave or sampling-strategy artifacts

These features may correlate with human preferences in the training data but are not necessarily causal determinants of response quality.

#### Supporting evidence

For PRISM, model identity is reported as the strongest determinant of counterfactual memorization. The paper observes that:

- RMs assign systematically different scores to responses from different source models.
- Model-wise RM preferences are positively correlated with average human preference.
- Violations of learned model-specific preferences cause particularly large degradation on unseen pairs.
- When both responses come from the same model, RM performance drops substantially.

For COMMUNITY, user metadata has high counterfactual-memorization importance. In particular:

- Preference pairs involving users in the “at most complete secondary” education group show a large reduction in performance on unseen pairs.
- These users were predominantly collected during a second recruitment wave designed to increase representation of less-educated participants.
- The authors argue that the RM may be memorizing recruitment-wave-associated user traits through prompts and preference patterns.

#### Assumptions

The causal interpretation depends on several assumptions:

- Model identity and user metadata are not causally related to response quality.
- The relevant metadata are available to, or inferable by, the RM from the conversation and response text.
- The observed performance degradation is not due to semantic or topic differences between subgroups.
- Recruitment wave is the primary explanation for the education-group effect.
- Model-specific behavior can be separated from genuine differences in response quality.

The reported evidence is suggestive but does not establish that the RM is using model identity or recruitment artifacts as causal shortcuts. Model identity may be recoverable because different models have systematic stylistic, safety, formatting, or capability differences. A model that scores these differences may be exploiting a legitimate quality signal, an illegitimate source-identification signal, or both.

### Argument 3: RMs overgeneralize simple heuristic correlates of human preference

#### Claim

When preference pairs are unseen, RMs overgeneralize superficial correlates such as:

- Response length and complexity
- Compliance
- Formatting
- Refusal-related language
- User-frame adoption or sycophancy-related behavior

The RM applies these heuristics even when they are inappropriate for the particular prompt.

#### Supporting evidence

The paper reports that:

- Complexity and length differences are strong predictors of testing behavior.
- More verbose or complex responses are often preferred by the RM even when the preference is inappropriate.
- When the chosen response violates the learned length heuristic, the RM must rely more heavily on memorization.
- Compliance produces large positive effects on both training and testing predictions.
- Non-compliance and refusal-related features are more difficult to generalize.
- In PRISM, disclaimer and meta-commentary features are associated with high counterfactual memorization.
- “Framing,” described as unquestioningly adopting the user’s frame, is associated with high counterfactual memorization in both datasets.

The regression models achieve \(R^2 = 0.52/0.38\) for testing generalization and \(R^2 = 0.39/0.33\) for counterfactual memorization on PRISM/COMMUNITY, respectively.

#### Assumptions

This conclusion assumes that:

- The automated annotations accurately identify the relevant linguistic properties.
- Differences in length, complexity, compliance, and framing are not simply valid proxies for answer completeness or instruction-following.
- SHAP values from the regression models are interpreted as explanatory associations rather than causal effects.
- The features are sufficiently disentangled to identify a specific heuristic.

The evidence supports the existence of correlations, but not necessarily the claim that the RM “overgeneralizes” those features in a causal or behavioral sense. For example, a longer response may be preferred because it contains more relevant information. Establishing inappropriate length preference requires controlled counterfactual evaluations in which length is manipulated while semantic quality is held fixed.

### Argument 4: Preference-pair regions reveal qualitatively different types of RM behavior

#### Claim

The two-dimensional map formed by training memorization and testing generalization contains interpretable regions:

- High training memorization / high testing generalization: conventional, easy, heuristic-aligned examples
- High training memorization / low testing generalization: examples that violate learned heuristics and require memorization
- Medium training memorization / medium testing generalization: ambiguous or contested examples
- Low training memorization / low testing generalization: difficult or poorly represented cases

#### Supporting evidence

The authors train a sparse autoencoder on response-embedding differences and add a region-classification objective. LLM-generated descriptions of selected latent features are used to interpret the four regions.

For PRISM, the authors report that:

- High/high examples tend to contain long, neutral, maximally compliant responses.
- High/low examples contain short responses, sometimes one-word answers, that violate the length heuristic.
- Medium/medium examples often concern controversial or politically charged topics where multiple responses may plausibly be preferred.

For COMMUNITY, the regions are less clearly differentiated, although formatting and topic differences appear repeatedly.

#### Assumptions

This argument assumes that:

- The four regions are meaningful rather than artifacts of arbitrary binning.
- The learned sparse autoencoder features correspond to semantically coherent dimensions.
- The auxiliary region-classification loss does not artificially create region-discriminative features.
- LLM-generated feature descriptions accurately characterize the latent variables.
- Embedding differences from the base language model are a suitable representation of RM-relevant preference features.

This portion of the analysis is exploratory and should be presented as hypothesis generation rather than strong empirical validation.

## 3. Methodology

### Overall approach

The paper follows four main stages:

1. Train multiple reward models on random subsets of two preference datasets.
2. Estimate training memorization, testing generalization, and counterfactual memorization for each preference pair.
3. Regress these quantities against manually designed and metadata-derived features.
4. Use sparse autoencoders to discover additional data-driven features associated with regions of the memorization map.

### Datasets

The paper uses:

#### PRISM

- Approximately 1,400 users
- Approximately 8,000 conversations
- Approximately 27,200 turns
- Approximately 57,900 preference pairs
- First turns contain, on average, 3.75 responses from 21 models
- Later turns generally contain two responses from the same model
- Rich user, conversation, turn, and response metadata

#### COMMUNITY

- Approximately 770 users
- Approximately 17,800 conversations
- Approximately 45,800 turns
- Approximately 137,000 preference pairs
- Four responses per turn, generally from the same model
- A balanced English-only subset is used
- Less metadata than PRISM

The datasets differ substantially in response-model diversity, number of responses per turn, user composition, and metadata availability. This is useful for studying dataset-specific effects, but it also complicates direct comparison between the datasets.

### Reward-model training

The authors:

- Use Llama-3.2-1B as the backbone.
- Apply an LM → SFT → RM pipeline.
- Train discriminative RMs using a Bradley–Terry preference objective.
- Use LoRA for parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
- Train 25 models on randomly sampled subsets.
- Ensure that each preference pair appears in 20 subsets and is held out from 5 subsets.
- Split at the user level to reduce preference leakage.

For a preference pair \((x,y^+,y^-)\), the RM probability is:

\[
p(y^+ \succ y^-)
=
\sigma(r_m(y^+|x)-r_m(y^-|x)).
\]

### Counterfactual memorization

The paper defines:

\[
CM = TM - TG,
\]

where:

- \(TM\) is the expected correct-classification probability when the preference pair is included in training.
- \(TG\) is the expected correct-classification probability when it is held out.

Each pair is evaluated under both inclusion and exclusion conditions across the 25 trained RMs.

This produces a memorization map with:

- \(TM\) on the horizontal axis
- \(TG\) on the vertical axis
- \(CM\) increasing toward the lower-right corner

### Hypothesis-driven feature analysis

The authors annotate responses with 84 features covering:

- Anthropomorphism
- Complexity
- Dialog acts
- Emotion
- Politeness
- Rule-based rewards
- Sycophancy

They supplement these with dataset metadata and convert response-level features into chosen-versus-rejected differences.

A three-layer MLP predicts \(TM\), \(TG\), or \(CM\). SHAP values are then used to estimate feature contributions. The authors summarize feature importance using:

- Mean Absolute Value (MAV)
- Harmonic Mean Rank (HMR)

### Data-driven feature analysis

The authors:

- Divide the memorization map into four regions.
- Compute embedding differences between chosen and rejected responses using the language-model backbone.
- Train a Matryoshka Batch Top-k sparse autoencoder with up to 32 latent variables.
- Use an auxiliary region-classification loss.
- Generate textual descriptions of latent features using GPT-5.
- Validate feature descriptions using GPT-5-mini annotations.
- Retain descriptions passing correlation and Bonferroni-corrected significance tests.

## 4. Review

### Overall Score

## Major Revision

The paper presents a timely and potentially valuable framework for analyzing reward-model memorization. The use of counterfactual memorization maps to connect individual preference examples with learned shortcuts is novel and intellectually appealing. The results are also consistent with important concerns about reward-model reliance on length, compliance, model identity, and dataset artifacts.

However, the current evidence is not sufficient to support the paper’s strongest claims. The analysis is largely correlational, the train/test counterfactual construction introduces possible distribution-shift confounds, the feature-regression and SHAP methodology is under-validated, and the sparse-autoencoder analysis is highly exploratory. The paper would benefit from substantial additional experiments and a more careful distinction between memorization, shortcut learning, interpolation, and legitimate generalization.

The paper is promising but not yet ready for acceptance at a top-tier conference in its current form.

## Strengths

- The paper addresses an important and underexplored problem: memorization in reward models rather than only memorization in generative language models.
- Counterfactual memorization is a useful conceptual tool for separating training performance from held-out performance at the preference-pair level.
- The memorization-map visualization is intuitive and potentially useful for data curation and reward-model diagnostics.
- The analysis covers both hypothesis-driven and data-driven feature discovery.
- Using two datasets with different response-model and metadata structures helps expose dataset-specific effects.
- The paper identifies several practically relevant phenomena:
  - Length and complexity shortcuts
  - Compliance and refusal asymmetries
  - Model-identity dependence
  - Potential user-sampling artifacts
  - Difficulty handling context-dependent or contested preferences
- The paper appropriately acknowledges that memorization is not always harmful and that memorization can sometimes support generalization.
- The code release improves reproducibility in principle.

## Weaknesses and Detailed Critique

### 1. The counterfactual split may confound memorization with user-level distribution shift

The paper holds out preference pairs by holding out users. This is reasonable for avoiding direct user-preference leakage, but it means that the \(TG\) condition is not simply “the same example was not seen.” It is also a condition in which the user, their conversational style, their topics, and potentially their demographic or recruitment characteristics are absent from training.

Consequently:

\[
CM = TM - TG
\]

may capture a mixture of:

- Example-level memorization
- User-level distribution shift
- Conversation-level distribution shift
- Recruitment-wave differences
- Changes in the distribution of prompts and topics

This is especially important because the paper’s main claims concern user metadata and sampling strategy. If all examples from a user are removed together, then the analysis cannot cleanly distinguish memorization of an individual preference pair from inability to generalize to that user population.

The paper should report additional splitting schemes:

- Pair-level holdout with explicit user-overlap controls
- Conversation-level holdout
- User-level holdout
- Cross-recruitment-wave holdout
- Splits stratified by model identity, topic, and response length

The results should be compared across these schemes. Without this analysis, the interpretation of \(CM\) as instance-level memorization is too strong.

### 2. The relationship between preference margin and memorization is partly circular

The paper states that the preference margin is included in the PRISM reward-model training objective. If the loss explicitly weights or otherwise uses preference margin, then finding that preference margin predicts \(TM\) is expected from the training design.

This weakens the claim that the RM independently “misallocates memorization” to high-margin examples. The result may instead demonstrate that the training objective emphasizes those examples.

The authors should:

- Clearly specify the exact margin-weighting procedure.
- Train an otherwise identical RM without margin weighting.
- Compare standard Bradley–Terry training with margin-aware training.
- Report whether the same effect appears when preference margin is excluded from the objective.
- Control for label consistency, prompt difficulty, response-model identity, and the number of candidate responses.

The phrase “misallocation of memorization capacity” also requires a stronger operational definition. The experiments do not establish that the model has a fixed memorization capacity or that memorizing high-margin examples prevents learning other examples.

### 3. Counterfactual memorization is not equivalent to shortcut learning

A large difference between training and held-out performance indicates a membership-sensitive performance difference. It does not by itself establish that the model has memorized a spurious feature.

For example, a rare but legitimate preference pattern may be difficult to infer from other data and therefore show high \(CM\). Likewise, a model may generalize poorly because the held-out examples are semantically unusual rather than because it has learned an undesirable shortcut.

The paper should distinguish among:

- Instance memorization
- User or group memorization
- Feature-based shortcut learning
- Label noise
- Distribution shift
- Rare-event generalization failure
- Legitimate learning of rare contextual rules

Controlled interventions are needed. Candidate analyses include:

- Counterfactual edits that change only model identity.
- Length-normalized response comparisons.
- Controlled paraphrases preserving semantic content.
- Prompt-response swaps.
- Style transfer while holding factual content fixed.
- Human evaluation of pairs selected from high-\(CM\) regions.
- Direct causal mediation or ablation tests on suspected shortcuts.

### 4. Model identity may be a valid quality signal, not necessarily a spurious shortcut

The PRISM analysis finds that model identity strongly predicts RM behavior. This is interesting, but the conclusion that model identity is an illegitimate dataset-specific shortcut is not established.

Different response models may differ systematically in:

- Factuality
- Safety
- Instruction following
- Refusal behavior
- Formatting
- Reasoning ability
- Calibration
- Verbosity
- Topic coverage

An RM that learns a model-specific prior may be using a real quality difference. The problem is not model identity per se, but whether the model identity is used inappropriately when response content contradicts the prior.

The authors should evaluate model-identity effects under controlled conditions:

- Same response content rendered in different model-specific styles.
- Responses from the same model with quality manipulated.
- Responses from different models matched for length, style, and semantic quality.
- Cross-model and cross-dataset transfer.
- Explicit model-identity removal or anonymization.
- Evaluation on newly generated responses from models absent from training.

The current result should be described as “model-identity dependence” rather than definitive evidence of spurious memorization.

### 5. Automated feature annotation introduces substantial measurement error

The 84 features are generated using a combination of task-specific and general-purpose LLM annotators. The paper does not provide enough information in the main text about:

- Annotator prompts
- Inter-annotator agreement
- Annotation calibration
- Error rates
- Whether annotators saw the preference labels
- Whether annotations were independently validated by humans
- Stability across annotator models and random seeds

Feature errors are especially concerning for nuanced concepts such as:

- Sycophancy
- Framing
- Compliance
- Emotion
- Dialog acts
- Anthropomorphism
- Safety-related behavior

If the annotations are noisy or correlated with response length and formatting, then the regression results may reflect annotator artifacts rather than RM behavior.

The authors should report human validation on a representative sample, annotation agreement, robustness to alternative annotators, and performance using only objectively measurable features.

### 6. SHAP values are treated too strongly as explanations

The regression model predicts \(TM\), \(TG\), and \(CM\), after which SHAP is used to attribute predictions to features. This provides a useful descriptive analysis, but it does not show that a feature caused the RM’s behavior.

There are several technical concerns:

- The feature set is highly correlated.
- Conditional SHAP values depend on the background distribution and feature-dependence assumptions.
- SHAP explanations for an MLP explain the regression model, not the original reward model.
- The regressors may learn dataset-specific correlations.
- The use of 1,000 perturbation examples may make global summaries unstable.
- There is no clear uncertainty estimate for MAV or HMR.
- The paper does not report sensitivity to architecture, seed, background set, or feature removal.

The paper should include:

- Bootstrap confidence intervals for feature importance.
- Permutation importance and leave-one-block-out ablations.
- Simpler baseline models such as linear regression, generalized additive models, and random forests.
- Feature-correlation diagnostics.
- Stability analysis across reward-model seeds and subset draws.
- Explicit language that SHAP values are associative explanations.

### 7. The regression performance does not by itself validate the proposed explanation

The reported \(R^2\) values are moderately high, but the feature regressors are evaluated on random held-out preference pairs. Depending on the split, pairs from the same users or conversations may appear in both training and evaluation sets. This can inflate performance through user- and conversation-specific correlations.

The paper should report regression performance under:

- Pair-level splits
- Conversation-level splits
- User-level splits
- Recruitment-wave splits
- Cross-dataset transfer

It should also provide comparisons with feature blocks removed one at a time. The most important question is not only whether the features predict \(CM\), but whether the identified features explain out-of-distribution failures.

### 8. The sparse-autoencoder analysis is underpowered and potentially self-confirming

The SAE uses only a maximum of 32 latent variables, with approximately four active per sample. It is trained not only to reconstruct response-embedding differences but also to classify the four memorization-map regions. Therefore, the auxiliary classifier explicitly encourages the latents to encode the region labels that are later used to interpret the latents.

This creates a risk of circularity:

1. The data are divided into regions based on \(TM\) and \(TG\).
2. The SAE is trained to classify those regions.
3. The learned features are then interpreted as explaining the regions.

The resulting associations may be useful for visualization, but they are not independent evidence that the discovered features caused memorization.

Additional concerns include:

- The region boundaries are not clearly specified.
- The regions may be highly imbalanced.
- The SAE is trained on backbone embedding differences rather than RM internal representations.
- The reconstruction quality and sparsity-quality tradeoff are not reported.
- Results are qualitative and appear sensitive to LLM-generated descriptions.
- GPT-generated descriptions may suffer from post-hoc semantic rationalization.
- Significance testing after feature selection and textual-description selection may not have the stated validity.

The SAE section should be reframed as exploratory unless supported by independent interventions. The authors should report reconstruction metrics, latent stability, multiple SAE seeds and widths, region balance, and classifier performance. Ideally, discovered features should be tested using controlled perturbations.

### 9. The empirical scope is narrow

The study uses one 1B-parameter Llama backbone and LoRA-based reward-model training. This raises questions about whether the observed effects are properties of reward modeling generally or of this particular small, parameter-efficient setup.

The paper should include at least some of:

- Full-parameter fine-tuning
- Multiple backbone sizes
- Different model families
- Different LoRA ranks and target modules
- Different regularization settings
- Different training-set sizes
- Multiple preference-loss formulations
- A comparison with an implicit reward model such as DPO
- Results from larger or production-scale reward models

The use of LoRA may materially affect memorization. The paper mentions an appendix discussion of PEFT, but the main conclusions should not depend on omitted details.

### 10. No direct connection is shown to reward hacking or downstream RLHF behavior

The introduction motivates the work using reward hacking and undesirable behaviors in aligned chat models. However, the experiments measure RM classification behavior only. They do not show that high-\(CM\) features cause downstream optimization failures.

A stronger paper would connect the memorization maps to downstream consequences by:

- Optimizing a policy against the trained RM.
- Measuring whether policies exploit features associated with high \(CM\).
- Comparing reward-model scores with independent human judgments.
- Testing whether removing high-\(CM\) examples or features improves downstream policy quality.
- Measuring reward overoptimization as policy optimization steps increase.
- Evaluating safety and helpfulness under adversarial prompting.

Without such experiments, the paper’s claims about implications for safe and fair chat-model development remain plausible but indirect.

### 11. The human-preference interpretation is too broad

The paper concludes that discriminatively trained RMs are “not yet capable of judging response quality in context-dependent scenarios.” The reported experiments demonstrate failures on particular datasets and feature configurations, but they do not establish this broad conclusion.

The results could be stated more precisely:

- These RMs exhibit measurable dependence on dataset-specific features.
- They have difficulty generalizing some context-dependent preference distinctions.
- Their behavior is not reliably invariant to superficial changes in response properties.

The stronger claim about general inability to judge contextual quality requires broader evaluation across domains, prompts, principles, and human judges.

### 12. Dataset and evaluation details are insufficient in the provided main text

Several details appear to be deferred to appendices:

- Exact data-splitting and overlap controls
- Training-set sizes and sampling ratios
- Number of optimization steps
- LoRA configuration
- Learning rates and batch sizes
- Early stopping and model selection
- Number of random seeds
- Preference-margin construction
- Annotator prompts and validation
- Exact region boundaries
- Feature-block dimensions and preprocessing
- Regression hyperparameters
- SAE hyperparameters and stability

These details are essential for evaluating whether the reported effects are robust or artifacts of a particular implementation. They should be summarized in the main paper, at least sufficiently to make the central experiments reproducible.

## Recommendations

### Essential revisions

1. Clarify the counterfactual estimand.

   Precisely define whether \(CM\) measures example-level memorization, user-level memorization, or a mixture. Report results under pair-, conversation-, and user-level holdouts.

2. Address the preference-margin circularity.

   Include reward models trained without margin weighting and compare them with the current objective. Explain whether the margin is used as a loss weight, target, sampling variable, or auxiliary input.

3. Add causal or intervention-based shortcut tests.

   Manipulate length, formatting, model identity, compliance, refusal language, and user framing while preserving the underlying response content as much as possible.

4. Validate the automated annotations.

   Report human agreement and annotator-model robustness. Include analyses with objective features only.

5. Strengthen the SHAP analysis.

   Add confidence intervals, feature-block ablations, alternative regressors, and stability tests. Clearly distinguish predictive explanation from causal explanation.

6. Make the SAE analysis independently testable.

   Report reconstruction quality and latent stability, remove or separately analyze the region-classification auxiliary loss, and evaluate discovered features with controlled interventions.

7. Expand the reward-model ablations.

   Test multiple seeds, training sizes, LoRA configurations, and at least one alternative backbone or full fine-tuning setup.

8. Connect memorization to downstream behavior.

   Demonstrate whether high-\(CM\) examples or features actually lead to reward hacking during policy optimization or correlate with human-rated failures.

9. Moderate the conclusions.

   Limit claims to the two datasets and training configurations studied unless broader evidence is added. Replace causal language such as “memorize recruitment strategy” with more cautious wording where appropriate.

### Presentation improvements

- Define all dataset-map regions mathematically and state their sample counts.
- Report uncertainty for \(TM\), \(TG\), \(CM\), regression coefficients, and feature-importance summaries.
- Include a table of train/test performance by model identity and user subgroup.
- Provide examples of individual preference pairs from each memorization region.
- Show whether the same patterns persist after controlling for response length and model identity.
- Distinguish “high RM score,” “correct preference classification,” and “memorization” consistently.
- Explain why the probability of correct classification is preferred over classification accuracy and how calibration affects the results.
- Discuss whether negative \(CM\) values reflect noise, regularization, or beneficial training effects rather than simply dismissing them as noise.
- Include a clear limitations section covering dataset bias, annotator bias, model scale, PEFT, and the absence of downstream policy optimization.

## Overall Assessment

This paper has a strong and relevant research question and introduces a potentially useful diagnostic framework for reward models. Its central empirical observations are plausible and important: reward models can depend on response-model identity, user-sampling artifacts, compliance, and superficial response properties, while failing to generalize context-sensitive distinctions.

The main issue is evidential strength. The current methodology establishes predictive associations with counterfactual memorization, but it does not yet cleanly identify what the RM memorizes, establish that the identified features are spurious, or demonstrate downstream reward hacking. The user-level splitting strategy, margin-aware training objective, automated annotations, SHAP explanations, and region-supervised SAE all introduce possible confounds.

I therefore recommend Major Revision. With controlled interventions, stronger split and ablation studies, improved uncertainty analysis, and a direct connection to downstream reward optimization, the work could become a valuable contribution to the study of reward-model reliability.

## 1. Problem Statement

The paper studies instability in reinforcement-learning training for large language models caused by a mismatch between:

- The training policy `π`, computed by a training engine such as FSDP in BF16.
- The inference or rollout policy `μ`, computed by a separate inference engine such as vLLM using low-precision FP8 quantization.

The authors argue that this mismatch turns nominally on-policy RL into a form of off-policy learning. The resulting discrepancy can accumulate during training and cause instability or collapse. Existing approaches either:

1. Apply token-level or sequence-level importance sampling corrections, which introduce bias or high variance; or
2. Align training and inference precision, which reduces efficiency or may reduce accuracy.

The proposed objective is therefore to control the discrepancy adaptively rather than eliminate it. The central thesis is that the discrepancy should remain within a “reasonable range”: too large a discrepancy destabilizes training, while too small a discrepancy allegedly restricts the optimization space and reduces exploration or final accuracy.

## 2. Main Arguments

### Argument 1: Training-inference discrepancy is a major cause of RL instability

**Claim.** Differences between training and inference engines, especially FP8 inference versus BF16 training, produce a policy mismatch that can cause RL training collapse.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The paper gives a conceptual explanation based on the mismatch between samples drawn from `μ` and gradients computed using `π`.
- It cites prior work on train-inference mismatch and importance sampling.
- The experimental section claims that standard FP8 training becomes unstable, although the supplied text does not include the complete experimental results.

**Assumptions and concerns.**

- The paper treats the discrepancy as a primary causal factor, but does not isolate quantization error from other differences between vLLM and FSDP, such as attention kernels, sampling implementations, batching, reduction order, logits processing, and generation settings.
- The manuscript does not establish whether the observed collapse is caused by the mismatch itself, by FP8-specific numerical effects in logits or normalization, or by a broader systems issue.
- No controlled experiment is shown in the provided text where the engine is held fixed and only quantization is changed, or where quantization is held fixed and only backend differences are changed.

### Argument 2: The discrepancy should not simply be minimized

**Claim.** Driving the training-inference discrepancy toward zero can harm accuracy because it constrains the feasible parameter space and limits exploration.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The paper formulates constrained optimization with `E <= E0` and observes that a smaller bound yields a smaller feasible parameter set.
- It argues that a discrepancy penalty can conflict with maximizing the RL objective.
- It invokes the relation between token probability, advantage, and entropy.

**Assumptions and concerns.**

- The feasible-set argument is mathematically true but does not establish that lower train-inference discrepancy is harmful in the actual RL problem. The inference policy is not an independent regularizer or a desired constraint; it is the behavior policy used to collect samples. A lower mismatch may instead improve the validity of the policy-gradient estimate.
- The existence of an optimal parameter with `E* > E0` is asserted hypothetically, not demonstrated empirically.
- The claim that reducing discrepancy necessarily increases exploration is not established. Entropy depends on the full distributional update, not only on whether individual token probabilities are above or below their inference counterparts.
- The reference value is defined from the initial model, but there is no evidence that the initial discrepancy is an accuracy-preserving or exploration-preserving target.

### Argument 3: Advantage-guided probability adjustment can reduce discrepancy while preserving the RL direction

**Claim.** The update should be amplified or attenuated according to both the advantage sign and the relative ordering of `π` and `μ`.

**Supporting evidence.**

The paper describes four quadrants:

- Positive advantage and `π < μ`: amplify the positive update.
- Positive advantage and `π > μ`: reduce the positive update.
- Negative advantage and `π > μ`: amplify the negative update.
- Negative advantage and `π < μ`: reduce the negative update.

This is intended to preserve the advantage-driven update direction while moving the training probability toward the inference probability.

**Assumptions and concerns.**

- The quadrant logic is intuitive locally, but no aggregate analysis shows that these token-level adjustments decrease a well-defined policy divergence.
- Policy probabilities are coupled through the softmax normalization. Increasing the probability of one token affects all other tokens, so local reasoning about a single token does not directly imply a reduction in distributional discrepancy.
- The method operates on sampled tokens rather than the complete next-token distribution. It is therefore unclear whether the local correction controls the policy mismatch over unobserved but high-probability actions.
- The paper does not specify how zero, underflowed, or extremely small inference probabilities are handled.

### Argument 4: ACRL adaptively controls discrepancy around an initial reference value

**Claim.** ACRL uses a sequence-level discrepancy measurement to determine whether to increase or decrease the magnitude of token-level policy updates.

**Supporting evidence.**

The proposed method defines:

\[
d(a_{i,t},\theta_{\mathrm{old}})
=
\left|
\pi(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t},\theta_{\mathrm{old}})
-
\mu(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t},\theta_{\mathrm{old}})
\right|,
\]

an initial reference value `X`, a current sequence discrepancy `Y`, and an adaptive exponent:

\[
\alpha
=
\gamma \cdot \operatorname{sign}(A_{i,t})(1-Y/X).
\]

The correction is:

\[
\rho_{i,t}
=
\left(
\frac{\pi(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t},\theta_{\mathrm{old}})}
{\mu(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t},\theta_{\mathrm{old}})}
\right)^\alpha.
\]

The corrected gradient is weighted by `min(ρ_i,t, C)`.

The paper claims that this remains stable under aggressive FP8 inference and matches the BF16 baseline while outperforming TIS and MIS.

**Assumptions and concerns.**

- `Y` is computed at the current sequence level, while `X` is an average over a baseline dataset. This creates a potentially noisy and poorly calibrated controller.
- The method has no explicit integral control, moving average, target band, or aggregate feedback mechanism. When `Y < X`, the correction reverses direction and increases the mismatch; when `Y > X`, it decreases it. This may oscillate around `X`, particularly because `Y` varies substantially across prompts and response lengths.
- The paper does not show that the controller actually maintains discrepancy near `X`, as opposed to merely modifying performance-correlated gradients.
- The denominator `X` can be very small, causing an excessively large adaptive exponent. The manuscript does not specify safeguards for `X ≈ 0`.
- The phrase “tokens with a large `d` receive a stronger adjustment” is not supported by Equations (11)–(12). The exponent `α` is based on sequence-level `Y`, not token-level `d(a_i,t)`. Token-specific variation enters through the probability ratio, but the method does not explicitly scale the correction by each token’s discrepancy magnitude.
- The correction can be numerically unstable when `π/μ` is very large or very small. Only an upper clipping operation is specified, and the behavior of log-probability computation and finite-precision exponentiation is not described.
- The method is knowingly biased. The paper appropriately acknowledges this, but the comparison with MIS requires a more careful bias-variance analysis than the current discussion provides.

### Argument 5: ACRL improves exploration and accuracy

**Claim.** ACRL’s discrepancy-reduction mechanism preferentially increases the contribution of positive-advantage low-probability and negative-advantage high-probability tokens, thereby increasing entropy and exploration.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The stochastic-dominance claims in Equations (13) and (14) are unusually strong. A plotted density map is insufficient evidence for inequalities claimed for every threshold `P0 ∈ (0,1)`. The paper should provide empirical CDFs, confidence intervals, sample counts, and statistical tests.
- The analysis appears based on one Qwen2.5-3B setup. It is not clear whether the claimed ordering holds across models, tasks, training stages, response lengths, or quantization schemes.
- Relative probability ordering (`π > μ`) is not equivalent to absolute low/high probability. The paper acknowledges this distinction but then uses relative regions to support conclusions about exploration. The inference from relative ordering to entropy behavior remains incomplete.

## 3. Methodology

### Overall approach

ACRL modifies the GRPO gradient using an additional adaptive weight. The method retains the standard GRPO importance ratio:

\[
r_{i,t}
=
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{i,t}\mid q,a_{i,<t})},
\]

and introduces a train-inference correction:

\[
\rho_{i,t}
=
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}{\mu}\right)^{\alpha}.
\]

The correction is applied to the token gradient and clipped from above by `C`.

### Discrepancy metric

The discrepancy is the absolute difference between the training and inference probabilities of sampled tokens. It is averaged over response tokens and sampled responses.

This metric is computationally simple, but it is not a standard divergence between distributions. It ignores:

- Differences on nonsampled tokens.
- Relative error in low-probability events.
- Sequence-level likelihood ratios.
- The asymmetry between behavior-policy sampling and target-policy optimization.
- The impact of small per-token errors accumulating over long sequences.

### Adaptive controller

The baseline `X` is computed before training, using initial model weights. The current discrepancy `Y` is computed for each sampled sequence. The sign of `1 - Y/X` determines whether ACRL reduces or increases discrepancy relative to the advantage signal.

The parameter `γ` controls the magnitude, while `C` provides upper clipping.

### Baselines

The available text identifies the following baselines:

- BF16 training/inference alignment.
- FP8 inference without correction.
- Token-level importance sampling, TIS.
- Sequence-level masked importance sampling, MIS.
- ACRL with different values of `γ`.

The supplied text does not provide sufficient implementation details to determine whether these baselines were tuned comparably.

## 4. Review

### Overall Score

**Major Revision**

The paper presents a practically relevant systems problem and a simple, potentially useful heuristic for stabilizing RL under train-inference mismatch. The method is easy to implement and the reported results are promising. However, the central conceptual claims are not yet sufficiently justified, and the empirical evidence visible in the manuscript is not adequate for a top-tier AI/ML venue.

The main issue is not merely the acknowledged bias of ACRL. The deeper concern is that the method is described as a discrepancy controller, but the proposed feedback signal and update rule do not establish control of a well-defined policy divergence. Several conclusions about exploration, entropy, and accuracy rely on empirical assumptions that are stated much more strongly than they are validated.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important and increasingly relevant problem in LLM RL systems: the use of separate training and inference engines with different numerical precision.
- Identifies a real trade-off between low-precision inference efficiency, train-inference consistency, and RL stability.
- Provides a simple correction that can be integrated into GRPO-like algorithms without requiring full precision alignment.
- Explicitly discusses the bias-variance trade-off between token-level correction, sequence-level importance sampling, and adaptive biased weighting.
- The four-quadrant interpretation makes the intended update behavior relatively easy to understand.
- The use of FP8 inference with BF16 training reflects a realistic deployment and training configuration.
- The reported Qwen experiments suggest that ACRL may recover some of the accuracy lost by FP8 inference and may outperform the evaluated IS baselines.
- The paper attempts to connect training stability, entropy, exploration, and final accuracy rather than reporting accuracy alone.

### Weaknesses

#### 1. The discrepancy objective is weakly motivated

The paper uses average absolute probability differences on sampled tokens as its discrepancy metric. This is not a complete measure of mismatch between `π` and `μ`. Two distributions can have a small sampled-token absolute difference while differing substantially in KL divergence or in the probabilities of important unsampled actions. Conversely, absolute probability differences can be small for low-probability tokens despite large relative errors.

The choice of metric is therefore central, but the paper does not compare it against:

- Forward and reverse KL divergence.
- Total variation distance.
- Per-token log-probability difference.
- Sequence-level log-ratio.
- Maximum or high-quantile token discrepancy.

The claim that controlling `Y` controls the actual train-inference mismatch is consequently not established.

#### 2. The controller is not demonstrated to be stable

ACRL is called an adaptive control method, but the manuscript does not provide a control-theoretic or empirical demonstration that its feedback loop converges or remains within a target range.

Potential issues include:

- The target `X` is fixed at initialization.
- `Y` is measured per sequence and may be highly noisy.
- The controller reverses direction whenever `Y` crosses `X`.
- No smoothing, dead band, hysteresis, or batch-level aggregation is described.
- The update acts indirectly through policy gradients, so the relationship between correction strength and future discrepancy is neither monotonic nor immediate.

At minimum, the paper should report the trajectory of aggregate discrepancy, its variance, its distribution across prompts, and its relationship to training collapse. A plot of the mean alone would not be sufficient.

#### 3. The reference value `X` has no demonstrated semantic meaning

The initial discrepancy is used as the desired operating point. This choice is convenient, but there is no reason that the initial model’s discrepancy is optimal for later stages of RL. Quantization error can change with model weights, token distributions, activation ranges, response lengths, and task difficulty.

The paper should test alternatives such as:

- A moving-average reference.
- A percentile-based target.
- A manually selected discrepancy band.
- A target calibrated to the onset of instability.
- Separate references by task, prompt class, response length, or training stage.

The claim that a clean initial baseline is inherently desirable is not enough to justify using it as the controller target.

#### 4. The exploration analysis overclaims what is shown

The manuscript moves from an empirical relationship between `π > μ` and absolute token probability to claims about entropy and exploration. This involves several unsupported steps:

- Relative high/low probability is not the same as absolute high/low probability.
- The stochastic-dominance inequalities are asserted without statistical validation.
- The analysis appears model- and task-specific.
- The effect of token-level gradient scaling on the normalized policy distribution is not derived.
- Increased entropy is used as a proxy for better exploration, but higher entropy can also reflect degraded confidence or noisy optimization.

The entropy curve in Figure 8, even if valid, would establish only a correlation between ACRL and entropy. It would not demonstrate that the proposed quadrant mechanism causes the increase or that increased entropy causes the accuracy improvement.

#### 5. A key algorithmic explanation is inconsistent with the equations

The paper states that tokens with larger individual discrepancy `d(a_i,t, θ_old)` receive stronger adjustment. However, Equation (12) uses sequence-level `Y`, not token-level `d(a_i,t, θ_old)`. Equation (11) uses the token’s probability ratio, but that is not equivalent to weighting the correction by the magnitude of the absolute discrepancy.

This distinction should be corrected in the algorithm description. Either:

- The method should be described as sequence-level adaptive exponentiation with token-level probability ratios; or
- The algorithm should explicitly incorporate token-level discrepancy into `α` or `ρ`.

This is important because the claimed locality of the correction is part of the paper’s motivation.

#### 6. The causal source of instability is not isolated

The setup changes multiple factors simultaneously:

- Training engine.
- Inference engine.
- Precision.
- Kernel implementations.
- Runtime scheduling and batching.
- Possibly sampling and logits-processing behavior.

The paper needs controlled comparisons that separately evaluate:

- Same engine, BF16 versus FP8.
- Different engines with matched precision.
- Deterministic versus nondeterministic execution.
- Quantization-only perturbations.
- Kernel-only or backend-only mismatch.

Without these controls, it is difficult to attribute improvements specifically to ACRL’s discrepancy regulation.

#### 7. The empirical evidence is too narrow in the visible manuscript

The reported benchmarks are almost exclusively mathematical reasoning tasks, and only two model scales are shown in the supplied portion. The paper should establish whether the method applies to:

- Multiple model families.
- Multiple reward types.
- General instruction-following tasks.
- Code generation.
- Preference-based or verifier-based rewards.
- PPO, GRPO, DAPO, and other RL objectives.

The manuscript itself claims generality across algorithms and discrepancies, but the provided evidence does not yet support that breadth.

#### 8. Baseline comparisons are insufficiently specified

The excerpt does not report:

- The TIS truncation threshold.
- The MIS truncation threshold.
- GRPO clipping parameters.
- ACRL `γ` and `C` selection procedure.
- Learning rates and batch sizes.
- Number of random seeds.
- Sampling temperature and top-p.
- Whether each method receives equal rollout and training compute.
- Whether hyperparameters are tuned on the evaluation benchmarks.

This is particularly important because Table 3 shows a substantial difference between `γ = 0.35` and `γ = 0.65`. If ACRL requires tuning `γ` per task or benchmark, its practical advantage may be smaller than claimed.

#### 9. The reported improvements may not be statistically reliable

The benchmark sizes are small, especially AIME and HMMT. For example, a 3.33 percentage-point change may correspond to a single problem. The paper reports point estimates but no:

- Multiple-seed mean and standard deviation.
- Confidence intervals.
- Per-run collapse rates.
- Statistical significance tests.
- Evaluation variance due to sampling.

The claimed improvements over BF16 and MIS should be interpreted cautiously until run-level results are provided.

#### 10. The computational cost is not quantified

ACRL requires computing both training and inference probabilities for sampled tokens and applying an additional exponentiation and clipping operation. The paper claims practical efficiency but the supplied text contains no wall-clock, memory, throughput, or communication measurements.

The relevant comparison is not only against BF16. It should include:

- FP8 without correction.
- ACRL overhead relative to FP8.
- TIS overhead.
- MIS overhead.
- End-to-end time to reach a fixed accuracy.
- Additional memory and synchronization requirements.

Since the motivation is primarily systems efficiency, this omission is significant.

#### 11. Numerical edge cases are unspecified

The method uses `π/μ` and raises the ratio to an adaptive exponent. The paper should clarify:

- Whether probabilities are computed in log space.
- How zero or underflowed probabilities are handled.
- Whether the ratio is clipped before exponentiation.
- Whether `X` is lower-bounded.
- Whether `Y/X` is clipped.
- Whether negative or extreme `α` values are clipped.
- Whether the correction is applied before or after GRPO clipping.
- Whether gradients are detached through `ρ`.

These details can materially affect stability under FP8.

#### 12. The theoretical framing is incomplete

The paper provides a constrained optimization argument but no formal result connecting the proposed biased update to the constraint. There is no proof that:

- The expected discrepancy decreases when `Y > X`.
- The expected discrepancy increases when `Y < X`.
- The controller converges to a neighborhood of `X`.
- The biased update retains a useful descent or ascent property for the original RL objective.
- The correction avoids collapse under any stated assumptions.

A formal theorem is not strictly necessary for an empirical systems paper, but the paper should avoid presenting heuristic behavior as an established control principle without either a derivation or sufficiently strong empirical validation.

### Recommendations

1. **Define and justify the controlled quantity more carefully.** Compare absolute probability distance with KL, total variation, log-probability distance, sequence-level log-ratio, and quantile-based metrics. Explain which metric correlates with collapse.

2. **Provide a controller analysis.** Report batch-level and per-sequence discrepancy trajectories, variance, autocorrelation, oscillation behavior, and the effect of `γ`, `C`, and reference selection. Add a dead band or smoothing mechanism if the current controller oscillates.

3. **Correct the token-level description.** Clarify that the current equations use a sequence-level `Y` and token-level probability ratios. If token-local discrepancy control is intended, modify the equation accordingly and ablate the sequence-level and token-level variants.

4. **Perform causal ablations.** Separate quantization mismatch, engine mismatch, kernel mismatch, and runtime nondeterminism. Include same-engine and matched-precision controls.

5. **Strengthen the exploration evidence.** Report entropy, effective support size, action diversity, probability mass shifts, and exploration metrics across training. Validate the stochastic-dominance claims with empirical CDFs and confidence intervals.

6. **Evaluate across more settings.** Include more model families, at least one non-mathematical task, multiple reward types, and multiple RL algorithms. If the method is intended to generalize beyond FP8, test INT8, MXFP8, and other backend discrepancies.

7. **Report reproducibility details.** Include all hyperparameters, truncation constants, sampling settings, optimizer details, rollout batch sizes, precision formats, hardware, software versions, and seed counts.

8. **Report uncertainty and failure rates.** Provide mean and standard deviation over multiple runs, confidence intervals, the fraction of runs that collapse, and time-to-collapse distributions.

9. **Quantify end-to-end efficiency.** Measure throughput, rollout latency, training time, memory usage, communication overhead, and time-to-target-accuracy for all methods.

10. **Address numerical stability explicitly.** Compute ratios in log space, specify epsilon safeguards, clip `α` and `Y/X`, and document behavior when `μ` or `π` underflows.

11. **Include stronger baselines.** In addition to TIS and MIS, compare against precision alignment, shared-engine execution, explicit KL regularization, adaptive KL control, and a simple smoothed discrepancy controller.

12. **Temper the claims.** Until the missing experiments are supplied, conclusions should be limited to a heuristic that appears promising for FP8-based VeRL/GRPO training rather than a generally validated method for stable RL.

## Final Assessment

ACRL targets a consequential practical problem and proposes a simple mechanism with plausible empirical value. The core idea of adapting the strength of biased importance weighting according to observed train-inference mismatch is reasonable as an engineering heuristic. However, the current presentation conflates a sampled-token probability metric with full policy discrepancy, overstates the exploration interpretation, and does not demonstrate that the proposed feedback loop actually controls the discrepancy in a stable or principled way.

The paper could become a useful systems contribution after substantial revision, particularly with stronger causal ablations, multi-seed experiments, explicit numerical-stability details, rigorous controller diagnostics, and broader comparisons. Based on the supplied content, it is not ready for acceptance at a top-tier venue.

**Overall score: Major Revision**

## 1. Problem Statement

The paper studies constrained reinforcement learning (CRL), formulated as a constrained Markov decision process (CMDP). The central problem is that standard constrained RL algorithms typically learn a policy for a fixed cost function. When the safety-cost structure changes, for example because an obstacle moves or the operating configuration of a system changes, the policy and its future cost estimates may need to be retrained from scratch.

The paper proposes using successor representations to separate environment dynamics from reward and cost structures. The intended benefit is that changes to the reward or cost function can be handled by retraining only a low-dimensional reward/cost prediction component, while retaining the learned successor features that represent future state-action occupancy.

The motivating hypothesis is:

> If dynamics remain unchanged but the cost function changes, a successor-feature representation should permit substantially faster policy adaptation than a conventional value function that directly encodes the cost-augmented reward.

This is an important and practically relevant problem. However, the paper’s strongest claim is about rapid adaptation to changed cost functions, whereas the main baseline experiment described in the supplied text primarily evaluates final performance in static environments.

## 2. Main Arguments

### Argument 1: Successor representations can decouple dynamics from reward and cost structure

**Claim.** A successor representation can encode expected future state-action occupancy independently of the particular reward or cost function. Reward and cost values can then be recovered through linear weighting of the successor features.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The paper defines successor representations as discounted state occupancy.
- It uses a successor-feature approximation:
  \[
  Q^\pi(s,a) \approx M^\pi(s,a) \cdot w_r
  \]
- It introduces a corresponding cost predictor:
  \[
  C(s,a) \approx w_c \cdot \phi(s,a)
  \]
  and estimates future costs using the same successor features:
  \[
  K^\pi(s,a) \approx w_c \cdot M^\pi(s,a).
  \]

**Assumptions.**

- The relevant reward and cost functions can be represented sufficiently well as linear functions of the learned features.
- The environment dynamics remain fixed or change slowly enough that the successor features remain valid.
- The feature representation contains enough information to support both reward and cost prediction.
- The action-conditioned extension remains stable when the policy changes as a consequence of changing Lagrange multipliers.

The paper describes the linear representation assumption as “without loss of generality,” because the feature network could theoretically encode reward values directly. This is mathematically plausible with unrestricted representation capacity, but it is not without practical cost. In a finite-dimensional neural representation, approximation error, feature dimensionality, optimization dynamics, and interference between reward, cost, reconstruction, and successor losses are central empirical issues.

### Argument 2: SafeDSR adapts to changed costs more efficiently than SafeDQN

**Claim.** SafeDSR can adapt to changes in the cost distribution by updating the cost estimator and Lagrangian-related components, without retraining the complete value function.

**Supporting evidence.**

- SafeDQN learns directly from the cost-augmented reward:
  \[
  \widetilde R(s_t,a_t)=R(s_t,a_t)-\lambda C(s_t,a_t).
  \]
  Consequently, changes in either \(C\) or \(\lambda\) alter the target used by the Q-network.
- SafeDSR learns successor features independently from reward and cost weights.
- The Lagrangian multiplier is applied at decision time:
  \[
  \arg\max_a \{Q^\pi(s,a)-\lambda K^\pi(s,a)\}.
  \]
- The paper reports that SafeDSR adapts to changes in cost structure within a few environment interactions.

**Assumptions.**

- The new cost function is adequately covered by the collected state-action data.
- The successor features learned under the previous policy remain useful after the cost change.
- Re-estimating the cost weights is sufficient; changes in the policy do not require substantial updating of the successor representation.
- “Few interactions” is measured against an explicit and meaningful adaptation baseline.

This is the paper’s most interesting contribution, but it is also the least substantiated in the supplied material. The adaptation claim requires a carefully controlled experiment that compares sample efficiency after a cost shift, not merely final performance in a fixed environment.

### Argument 3: SafeDSR is competitive with existing constrained RL methods

**Claim.** SafeDSR is competitive with established constrained RL algorithms and performs particularly well in multi-room constrained navigation environments.

**Supporting evidence.**

The paper reports results after 500,000 environment steps using ten random seeds. In the reported cost environments:

- SafeDSR is the strongest method in the two-room and three-room settings according to safe goal count.
- SafeDSR obtains average safe goal rates above 60% in the two-room setting and above 30% in the three-room setting.
- In the one-room setting, several PID-Lagrangian methods outperform SafeDSR in final average safe goal rate.
- SafeDQN performs worse than SafeDSR at the reported training horizon in the more difficult environments.

**Assumptions and qualifications.**

- The custom grid environment is representative enough of constrained RL difficulty to support broader conclusions.
- The OmniSafe implementations and hyperparameters are comparable to the custom SafeDSR and SafeDQN implementations.
- The higher exploration rate used by SafeDSR and SafeDQN does not account for most of their advantage.
- Safe goal count and safe goal rate sufficiently represent the CMDP objective.

The results suggest that SafeDSR is effective in this particular navigation benchmark. They do not yet establish general competitiveness across domains, continuous actions, high-dimensional observations, multiple constraints, or environments where the transition dynamics also change.

### Argument 4: Safe goal count and safe goal rate are useful CRL metrics

**Claim.** Counting trajectories that reach the goal without violating the constraint gives an interpretable and robust measure for constrained goal-reaching tasks.

**Supporting evidence.**

The safe goal count is defined by whether a trajectory reaches the goal and satisfies all constraints. The authors argue that this metric:

- avoids directly balancing reward and cost;
- is more robust to outliers than average cost;
- remains more comparable when the environment changes;
- directly measures successful safe behavior.

**Limitations.**

This metric is appropriate for the specific goal-reaching task, but it is not a general replacement for CMDP metrics. The CMDP formulation constrains expected discounted cumulative cost, whereas the proposed metric applies a binary trajectory-level condition. A policy can have a low expected cost while frequently violating a threshold, or it can have a high expected cost due to rare severe failures while achieving a high safe-goal rate. These objectives are not interchangeable.

The safe-goal definition also appears to use the entire episode cost against the budget, while the formal CMDP uses discounted expected costs. The paper should state explicitly whether the experimental constraint is intentionally different from the formal objective.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 CMDP and Lagrangian relaxation

The paper formulates the CMDP as maximizing expected discounted reward subject to one or more expected discounted cost constraints.

It then applies Lagrangian relaxation:

\[
\max_\pi \min_{\lambda\geq 0}
\mathbb{E}_{p_\pi(\tau)}
\left[
\sum_t \gamma^t R(s_t,a_t)
-
\lambda \cdot
\left(
\sum_t \gamma^t C(s_t,a_t)-b
\right)
\right].
\]

The conventional SafeDQN baseline learns a Q-function for the cost-augmented reward. The Lagrange multiplier is updated at the end of each episode using a proportional rule:

\[
\lambda \leftarrow \max(0,\lambda+\alpha_\lambda(C_{\mathrm{ep}}-b)).
\]

### 3.2 SafeDQN

SafeDQN is a standard DQN-style architecture combined with Lagrangian relaxation.

Its key characteristics are:

- Q-learning on the cost-augmented reward;
- target network and experience replay;
- epsilon-greedy exploration;
- proportional update of the Lagrange multiplier;
- direct encoding of both reward and cost effects in the Q-function.

The stated weakness is that changes in the cost function or multiplier change the Q-learning target, so the whole Q-network must adapt.

### 3.3 SafeDSR

SafeDSR extends Deep Successor Representations to constrained RL.

The implementation contains:

- a feature network \(\phi(s,a)\);
- a successor-feature network \(M\);
- a reward predictor with weights \(w_r\);
- a cost predictor with weights \(w_c\);
- an inverse feature network \(g\);
- target networks for the successor features and feature representation;
- a replay buffer;
- biased sampling for rare reward and cost observations;
- a feature-freezing step after a specified number of environment interactions.

The state and action are concatenated, with the action represented using one-hot encoding. This allows reward and cost functions to depend on both state and action.

The feature loss combines:

1. reward prediction loss;
2. cost prediction loss;
3. reconstruction loss.

The successor-feature loss is a DQN-style temporal-difference objective:

\[
\left\|
\phi_{\mathrm{prev}}(s,a)
+
\gamma M_{\mathrm{prev}}(s',a')
-
M(\phi(s,a))
\right\|_2^2.
\]

The feature vectors are normalized to unit Euclidean norm. The feature network is frozen after a prescribed training time to stabilize successor-feature learning.

At action-selection time, the method computes reward and cost estimates from successor features and chooses the action maximizing the Lagrangian-adjusted value:

\[
Q^\pi(s,a)-\lambda K^\pi(s,a).
\]

This is conceptually clean: the learned occupancy representation is shared, while reward and cost heads provide separate linear evaluations.

### 3.4 Experimental environment

The main environment is a continuous-state, discrete-action two-dimensional navigation task.

- State: two-dimensional agent position.
- Actions: up, down, left, right.
- Step length: noisy Gaussian displacement, clipped to three standard deviations.
- Walls: invalid movement is undone.
- Reward: \(1\) on reaching the goal and \(-0.01\) per step.
- Cost: \(1\) for each step taken inside a cost cell.
- Budget: \(5\).
- Termination: reaching the goal or truncation after 1,000 steps.
- Difficulty: one-, two-, and three-room configurations.
- Evaluation: ten seeds and \(\gamma=0.99\).

The environment is useful for visualization and debugging, but it is small and structurally favorable to state-based successor representations. It does not test image observations, large state spaces, continuous actions, partial observability, stochastic cost dynamics, or realistic safety-critical failure modes.

## 4. Review

### Overall Score

**Major Revision**

The paper presents a plausible and potentially valuable application of successor representations to constrained RL. The core idea is technically reasonable, and the custom experiments appear to show meaningful gains in the tested multi-room navigation environment. However, the evidence supplied does not yet justify the broad adaptation claims or establish that SafeDSR’s advantages come from successor representations rather than exploration settings, implementation choices, or benchmark-specific properties.

For a top-tier AI/ML venue, the paper needs stronger controlled experiments, a clearer distinction between the formal CMDP objective and the proposed trajectory-level metrics, and substantially more analysis of the conditions under which successor features remain valid after policy and cost changes.

### Strengths

- **Relevant problem.** Rapid adaptation to changing safety constraints is important for real-world deployment.
- **Clear central idea.** The separation of dynamics from reward and cost evaluation through successor features is intuitive and technically motivated.
- **Natural constrained-RL formulation.** Applying Lagrangian relaxation only at decision time is a sensible use of reward/cost factorization.
- **Action-dependent formulation.** Extending the features to state-action pairs is necessary for general reward and cost functions and is a useful design choice.
- **Interpretable environment.** The navigation benchmark allows trajectory and value-function visualization.
- **Direct baseline.** SafeDQN is a reasonable comparison because it isolates the difference between a conventional Q-function and a successor-feature parameterization.
- **Multi-room results are suggestive.** The reported results indicate that SafeDSR can find safe routes in settings where several baselines struggle.
- **Attention to training robustness.** Target networks, replay, feature normalization, biased sampling, and feature freezing are practical mechanisms addressing known instability issues.

### Weaknesses and Technical Concerns

#### 1. The principal adaptation claim is insufficiently documented

The paper’s central contribution is rapid retraining under a changed cost function, but the supplied results mainly describe static benchmark comparisons. The manuscript needs precise information about:

- when the cost function changes;
- how many state-action cost observations are needed after the change;
- whether the new cost geometry is completely unseen;
- whether the cost change is a relocation, resizing, appearance, disappearance, or arbitrary remapping;
- whether the replay buffer contains old cost labels that become invalid;
- whether the feature network and successor network are frozen during adaptation;
- what exactly is retrained;
- how performance is measured immediately before and after the shift;
- which baselines receive the same adaptation protocol.

“Within a few environment interactions” is not a sufficiently precise scientific result. The paper should report adaptation curves with confidence intervals and normalize sample counts across methods.

#### 2. The successor representation is policy-dependent

The successor features \(M^\pi\) depend on the policy \(\pi\), not only on the environment dynamics. Changing the Lagrange multiplier or cost function changes the greedy policy. Therefore, the successor features learned under the old policy are not generally the exact successor features of the new policy.

This creates a fundamental tension in the proposed method:

- If the cost changes and the policy changes substantially, old successor features may no longer accurately estimate future costs under the new policy.
- If the successor network is updated online, adaptation still requires environment interactions and may not be limited to supervised cost-weight updates.
- If the successor network is frozen, the method may provide fast approximate re-ranking of previously represented behavior, but not exact evaluation of newly induced policies.

The paper should clearly characterize the regime in which SafeDSR is expected to work. A comparison against successor features trained under the old policy, a re-planning oracle, and a model-based method would clarify this issue.

#### 3. The “dynamics versus reward/cost” decomposition is overstated

The successor representation separates future occupancy from linear reward/cost weights, but it does not remove all dynamics-dependent learning. The successor representation itself is policy-conditioned and must reflect how actions lead to future states. Moreover, the learned feature basis is trained jointly with reward, cost, reconstruction, and successor objectives.

The claim that changing the cost only requires updating a single weight matrix is therefore conditional:

- the new cost must lie in the span of the learned feature representation;
- the feature basis must remain useful after the policy changes;
- the new cost must be observed or otherwise known;
- the state-action coverage must be adequate.

These conditions should be stated as limitations rather than presenting the adaptation as generally applicable to arbitrary new cost structures.

#### 4. Safe goal rate does not evaluate the formal CMDP constraint

The formal CMDP constrains expected discounted cumulative cost. The safe goal count instead treats a trajectory as safe if its observed cumulative cost is below the budget.

This introduces several discrepancies:

- expected discounted costs versus observed episode costs;
- binary trajectory safety versus the magnitude of violations;
- terminal success versus constraint satisfaction;
- possible differences between per-step cost and episode-level cost;
- possible differences between training trajectories and evaluation trajectories.

The metric is useful for this particular navigation task, but it should be reported alongside standard CRL quantities such as:

- mean episodic reward;
- mean episodic cost;
- constraint violation rate;
- probability of exceeding the budget;
- worst-case or high-quantile cost;
- success rate conditional on safety.

The paper should avoid suggesting that safe goal count is broadly preferable to numerical cost evaluation.

#### 5. The Lagrange update is inconsistent with the stated discounted objective

Equation (16) updates the multiplier using \(C_{\mathrm{ep}}-b\), while the CMDP objective in Equation (1) uses discounted cumulative cost. The manuscript should specify whether \(C_{\mathrm{ep}}\) is discounted. If it is not, the implementation optimizes a different constraint than the one formally defined.

There is also a mismatch between using a single episode’s cost and estimating an expected constraint. A proportional update based on one episode can be highly noisy, particularly in environments with stochastic movement and sparse cost cells. The paper should report sensitivity to multiplier learning rate, update frequency, smoothing, and delayed multiplier initialization.

#### 6. Baseline comparisons are confounded by exploration

The paper explicitly attributes some of SafeDSR’s performance to its high epsilon-greedy exploration rate of \(0.25\). This is a serious confound. If policy-gradient baselines use different exploration mechanisms or lower effective exploration, the comparison does not isolate the algorithmic value of successor representations.

The paper should include controls with:

- matched exploration budgets;
- SafeDQN and SafeDSR using identical epsilon schedules;
- policy-gradient baselines with tuned entropy coefficients;
- an exploration-only baseline;
- ablations showing performance as a function of epsilon.

The conclusion that SafeDSR outperforms other algorithms should be weakened unless this confound is addressed.

#### 7. SafeDSR contains several additional mechanisms that are not isolated

The proposed method differs from SafeDQN in more than its value parameterization. SafeDSR also uses:

- separate reward and cost heads;
- feature normalization;
- target features;
- biased sampling;
- feature freezing;
- potentially different learning targets and optimization schedules.

These may each affect performance. At minimum, the paper needs ablations for:

- no feature freezing;
- different freezing times;
- no biased sampling;
- reward-only versus reward-and-cost feature losses;
- different feature dimensions;
- no reconstruction loss;
- no feature normalization;
- shared versus separate target networks;
- cost prediction error versus downstream policy performance.

Without these experiments, the observed improvement cannot be attributed specifically to the successor representation.

#### 8. The feature representation may be unsuitable for arbitrary cost changes

The method uses a fixed feature network and a linear cost predictor. A cost function defined by a new obstacle configuration may have a sharp spatial discontinuity. Whether it can be represented accurately by a frozen feature basis depends on feature dimension, network capacity, training coverage, and the reconstruction objective.

The paper should report:

- cost-prediction error before and after a shift;
- feature dimension;
- the spatial resolution of the cost structure;
- generalization to cost cells not visited during training;
- adaptation performance for increasingly different cost maps;
- comparisons to directly learning a new cost head from scratch.

The statement that arbitrary unknown cost functions can be handled is too broad without such evidence.

#### 9. The benchmark is too limited for the scope of the claims

The environment has a two-dimensional state, four discrete actions, a single cost, a known goal, and simple spatial cost cells. This is a reasonable proof-of-concept, but it is not enough to establish general CRL utility.

Important missing settings include:

- multiple simultaneous cost constraints;
- continuous action spaces;
- high-dimensional observations;
- partial observability;
- non-spatial or history-dependent costs;
- stochastic or delayed costs;
- changing dynamics;
- large-scale environments;
- safety-critical catastrophic costs rather than unit cell penalties.

The paper should present the contribution as an initial proof of concept unless broader experiments are added.

#### 10. Statistical reporting appears insufficient in the supplied text

The figures reportedly average ten seeds and use interquartile means. Robust aggregation is useful, but it can obscure uncertainty and is not a substitute for reporting variability.

The paper should include:

- per-seed results;
- confidence intervals or standard errors;
- standard deviations in the main figures rather than only supplementary sections;
- statistical tests or effect-size estimates for key comparisons;
- exact training budgets and evaluation protocols;
- sensitivity to random initialization and environment generation.

Some reported counts are rounded to integers, which makes it difficult to assess whether differences are statistically meaningful.

#### 11. The claim that Lagrangian methods converge needs qualification

The statement that the formulation is guaranteed to converge to the optimal policy in the infinite-data limit because CMDPs have zero duality gap is too broad in context. Zero duality gap for an appropriate CMDP does not imply that the particular deep Q-learning algorithm, nonlinear function approximator, off-policy replay procedure, multiplier controller, and finite-data training process converges to the optimum.

The paper should distinguish:

- the theoretical duality property of the CMDP;
- convergence of exact optimization;
- convergence of tabular algorithms under assumptions;
- empirical behavior of SafeDQN and SafeDSR with nonlinear approximation.

#### 12. Reproducibility details need to be central and complete

The paper refers to an external repository and supplementary parameter sections. For reproducibility, the main paper or artifact should clearly provide:

- all network architectures;
- feature dimension;
- optimizer and learning rates;
- target-network update frequency;
- replay-buffer size;
- batch size;
- epsilon schedule;
- multiplier initialization and clipping;
- feature-freezing time;
- loss coefficients \(\beta_r,\beta_c,\beta_g\);
- environment layouts and randomization;
- evaluation episodes;
- exact baseline versions and modifications.

These details are especially important because the method has many interacting stabilization components.

## Recommendations

1. **Make the cost-shift experiment the primary experiment.** Define several controlled shifts, report pre-shift and post-shift performance, and measure the number of interactions needed to recover a specified fraction of performance.

2. **Compare adaptation protocols fairly.** Give SafeDQN, policy-gradient baselines, and model-based or objective-conditioned baselines the same post-shift observations and computational budget.

3. **Disentangle the source of improvement.** Add ablations for feature freezing, biased sampling, normalization, reconstruction, cost prediction, and exploration rate.

4. **Report both formal and task-specific safety metrics.** Include discounted expected cost, empirical violation probability, average reward, success rate, safe success rate, and cost quantiles.

5. **Clarify the policy-dependence of successor features.** Explain whether SafeDSR is intended to reuse a fixed representation for approximate action re-ranking or to continually update the successor network after policy changes. Provide empirical evidence for the chosen interpretation.

6. **Test representation generalization.** Evaluate cost changes that occur in unseen regions, cost maps with different spatial complexity, and changes that require substantial policy rerouting.

7. **Add multiple-constraint experiments.** The method is presented for \(J\) constraints, but the reported environment uses only one. Demonstrate whether the shared feature basis scales with several independent cost structures.

8. **Address the discounted-versus-undiscounted mismatch.** State precisely how episode cost is calculated for multiplier updates and evaluation, and align the implementation with the formal CMDP objective or explicitly define a different objective.

9. **Improve statistical analysis.** Show confidence intervals, per-seed distributions, and significance or effect-size information for the main comparisons.

10. **Reduce the scope of the conclusions.** Unless broader experiments are added, frame SafeDSR as a promising proof of concept for fast adaptation in low-dimensional navigation with fixed dynamics, rather than a generally flexible constrained-RL solution.

## Final Assessment

SafeDSR is a coherent extension of successor-feature methods to constrained reinforcement learning. The architecture makes a useful conceptual distinction between learning future occupancy and evaluating reward or cost functions, and the reported multi-room results indicate that this parameterization can be effective in a sparse, constrained navigation problem.

The current evidence is not yet sufficient for acceptance at a top-tier venue. The main contribution is rapid cost adaptation, but that claim needs a substantially more rigorous experimental treatment. In addition, the results are confounded by exploration choices and several SafeDSR-specific stabilization techniques, while the proposed safe-goal metrics do not directly correspond to the formal expected discounted CMDP constraint.

**Recommended decision: Major Revision.**
