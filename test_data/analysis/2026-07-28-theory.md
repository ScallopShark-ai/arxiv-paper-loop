## 2. Key Derivations

The paper presents no multi-step derivations. What follows are (a) audits of the four stated equations and (b) the derivations that are required to support the paper's claims, reconstructed here so the gaps are visible.

### 2.1 Eq. (1) — imitation under the student-induced distribution

$$J(\pi_S) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_S}}\!\big[\ell\big(\pi_T(s),\pi_S(s)\big)\big]$$

**Starting point:** behavioral cloning minimizes risk under $d^{\pi_T}$; the mismatch $d^{\pi_S}\neq d^{\pi_T}$ is covariate shift.
**Status:** correctly stated, and correctly motivated. This is the AggreVaTe/DAgger objective.
**What is missing:** the reason this reformulation matters is a horizon-dependent regret reduction — cloning under $d^{\pi_T}$ with per-step error $\varepsilon$ admits only an $O(\varepsilon T^2)$ bound on return loss, whereas optimizing under $d^{\pi_S}$ (with the Q-weighting of Eq. 2) reduces this to $O(\varepsilon T)$. The paper cites the objective but never states the bound, so the reader is given no quantitative reason to prefer it, and no $T$-dependence — despite $T=1000$ being the whole task.

### 2.2 Eq. (2) — Q-gap-weighted loss

$$L_k(\theta_S)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}_k}\Big[\max_{a'}Q^{\pi_T}(s,a')-Q^{\pi_T}\big(s,\pi_S(s;\theta_S)\big)\Big]$$

**Audit.** The integrand is the suboptimality gap; it is non-negative provided the $\max$ is taken over the same $\mathcal{A}$ as $\pi_S$'s range, so the objective is well-posed as written.

Three technical problems, none acknowledged:

1. **This is not VIPER's objective.** VIPER weights a *0-1 classification* loss by the gap, $\tilde\ell(s)=\big(V^{\pi_T}(s)-\min_a Q^{\pi_T}(s,a)\big)\cdot\mathbb{1}[\pi_T(s)\neq\pi_S(s)]$, and then *resamples* states proportionally to the weight. That reduction is the entire point: it converts an intractable value-based objective into a weighted classification problem that CART can actually minimize.
2. **Eq. (2) as written is not minimizable by the tool used.** `DecisionTreeRegressor` greedily minimizes within-node squared error against scalar targets. It has no mechanism to descend a $Q$-gap. Without the weight-then-classify reduction, Eq. (2) is decorative.
3. **VIPER's guarantee is for discrete action spaces.** Here $\mathcal{A}=[-3,3]$ is continuous and the student is a regressor, so $\max_{a'}Q^{\pi_T}(s,a')$ requires a continuous maximization that is never performed. Also, $\max_{a'}Q^{\pi_T}(s,a')\neq V^{\pi_T}(s)$ unless $\pi_T$ is greedy w.r.t. its own critic — approximately true for a converged TD3 actor, but an assumption that should be stated.

**Consequence.** §III-C describes a *single-pass* collection of noise-injected expert rollouts. There is no $k$, no aggregation, no student deployment during data collection, and no Q-weighting. Equations (1)–(2) describe an algorithm the paper does not run. The method actually implemented is noise-injected behavioral cloning (i.e. DART-style, Laskey et al. 2017), which mitigates covariate shift by *pre-inflating* the expert's state distribution rather than by iterating on $d^{\pi_S}$. That is a legitimate technique with its own analysis, but it is a different algorithm with weaker guarantees, and the paper's theory section points at the wrong one.

### 2.3 Eq. (3) — teacher objective

$$J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^t r_t\Big]$$

**Audit.** Correct in form, but under-specified and inconsistent with the reported results:

- The expectation's measure (trajectory distribution induced by $\pi_\phi$ and $P$) is not stated.
- $\gamma$ is never given. With $r_t\equiv 1$ and $T=1000$, the maximum of Eq. (3) is $\frac{1-\gamma^{1001}}{1-\gamma}$, which equals the reported "1000.0" only for $\gamma=1$. The reported metric is the undiscounted return; Eq. (3) is therefore not the quantity being measured.
- TD3's actual machinery — the clipped double-Q target $y=r+\gamma\min_{i\in\{1,2\}}Q_{\theta_i'}\!\big(s',\pi_{\phi'}(s')+\tilde\epsilon\big)$ with $\tilde\epsilon\sim\text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde\sigma^2),-c,c)$, and the deterministic policy gradient $\nabla_\phi J=\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_a Q(s,a)\vert_{a=\pi_\phi(s)}\nabla_\phi\pi_\phi(s)\big]$ — is described in prose ("clipped double-Q learning and delayed policy updates") but never written. The overestimation-bias claim is thus asserted, not shown.

### 2.4 Eq. (4) — Pole Urgency, and the derivation the paper should have done

$$P_{\text{urgency}}=\theta+K_d\dot\theta$$

The paper calls this a "predictive look-ahead metric" and says it produces "diagonal decision boundaries." Both statements are correct and both are provable in two lines, yet neither is derived. Supplying them:

**(a) It is exactly a first-order look-ahead.** Taylor expansion about $t$:
$$\theta(t+\tau)=\theta(t)+\tau\dot\theta(t)+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^2\ddot\theta(t)+O(\tau^3).$$
Setting $\tau=K_d$ gives $P_{\text{urgency}}=\theta(t+K_d)+O(K_d^2\ddot\theta)$. So **$K_d$ is a prediction horizon in seconds**, and the feature's validity degrades quadratically in $K_d$ with the angular acceleration. This immediately explains why $K_d$ matters and why it should be tuned against $\lvert\ddot\theta\rvert$ — a sensitivity analysis the paper does not run and a value it does not report.

**(b) It is a sliding surface with time constant $K_d$.** A tree split $P_{\text{urgency}}\le c$ is the half-space $\theta+K_d\dot\theta\le c$. On the switching surface $\sigma(s):=\theta+K_d\dot\theta=0$,
$$\dot\theta=-\frac{1}{K_d}\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta(t)=\theta_0 e^{-t/K_d}.$$
So the reduced-order dynamics constrained to the surface are exponentially stable with time constant exactly $K_d$. The paper's remark that the boundaries are "conceptually identical to Sliding Mode Control boundaries" (§IV-C) is not merely conceptual — it is an identity, with $\lambda=1/K_d$ in the standard SMC form $\sigma=\dot\theta+\lambda\theta$.

**(c) The hypothesis-space gain is narrower than claimed.** Adding one feature does *not* give the tree general oblique splits. The reachable split family becomes hyperplanes with normals in
$$\{e_x,e_v,e_\theta,e_\omega\}\cup\{(0,0,1,K_d)\},$$
i.e. axis-aligned cuts **plus a single fixed oblique orientation**. All oblique boundaries are mutually parallel, with slope fixed by $K_d$. This is a "one-direction oblique tree," which is why $K_d$ is effectively a hard-coded control gain rather than a learned parameter. The paper's claim that the feature "allows the surrogate model to approximate non-linear control logic" is true only along that one direction; any other required diagonal must still be staircase-approximated.

### 2.5 The BIBO argument the paper asserts but does not make

§II-D claims that "bounding the quantization error $e_q(s)$ [guarantees] BIBO stability." **This implication is false as stated** — bounded actuation error does not imply bounded state for a general nonlinear closed loop. The correct statement requires an input-to-state-stability (ISS) premise:

**Setup.** $s_{t+1}=f(s_t,a_t)$. Under the student, $a_t=\pi_S(s_t)=\pi_T(s_t)-e_q(s_t)$, so the student's closed loop is the *teacher's* closed loop driven by an additive disturbance $d_t=-e_q(s_t)$:
$$s_{t+1}=f\big(s_t,\pi_T(s_t)+d_t\big).$$

**Assumption (A1).** The teacher's closed loop is ISS w.r.t. additive actuation perturbation: there exist $\beta\in\mathcal{KL}$, $\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}\in\mathcal{K}$ with
$$\|s_t\|\le\beta(\|s_0\|,t)+\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}\big(\|d\|_\infty\big).$$

**Assumption (A2).** $\varepsilon_\infty:=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{R}}\lvert e_q(s)\rvert$ is finite on the student's reachable set $\mathcal{R}$.

**Conclusion.** $\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|s_t\|\le\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}(\varepsilon_\infty)$, and safety holds iff $\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}(\varepsilon_\infty)<\theta_{\text{fail}}$.

This is *ultimate boundedness*, not BIBO. Three gaps the paper would have to close:

- **(A1) is unverified.** $\pi_T$ is a 2×256 ReLU network and $f$ is MuJoCo contact-free but nonlinear; no Lyapunov function or ISS gain is computed. Note also that ISS w.r.t. actuation error *cannot* hold globally here — the pendulum has a finite basin of attraction under bounded force $\lvert a\rvert\le3$, so (A1) is at best local, and the region of validity is never characterized.
- **(A2) is circular as written.** $\mathcal{R}$ depends on $\pi_S$, which depends on the bound. Resolving this needs a fixed-point/invariant-set argument: find a set $\Omega$ with $\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}\big(\sup_{\Omega}\lvert e_q\rvert\big)$-sublevel contained in $\Omega$.
- **The empirical proxy measures the wrong norm on the wrong distribution.** §III-F estimates fidelity with MSE and $R^2$ — an $L_2$ average over an *offline dataset drawn under the teacher*. (A2) requires an $L_\infty$ bound over the *student's* reachable set. A small MSE is fully compatible with a large $\varepsilon_\infty$ concentrated exactly in the rare near-failure states that determine safety.

**Terminology.** BIBO stability is an input–output property of (typically LTI) systems. Here no exogenous input is defined, the plant is nonlinear, and the demonstrated property is finite-horizon trajectory boundedness from a particular initial distribution over 50 episodes. "BIBO" is the wrong label; "practical stability / ultimate boundedness" is the right one. And 50 episodes of 1000 steps is evidence, not a proof — the phrase "mathematically proves" (§IV-B) is not defensible.

### 2.6 Why the steady-state angle histogram is bimodal (derivable; not derived)

The paper treats bimodality as evidence of a limit cycle but does not explain the mechanism. For a periodic orbit, the time-occupancy density of $\theta$ is inversely proportional to speed:
$$p(\theta)\;\propto\;\frac{1}{\lvert\dot\theta(\theta)\rvert},$$
which diverges at the turning points where $\dot\theta=0$. For a near-sinusoidal orbit $\theta(t)=A\sin\omega t$ this yields the arcsine law
$$p(\theta)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{A^2-\theta^2}},\qquad \lvert\theta\rvert<A,$$
with peaks precisely at $\theta=\pm A$. So bimodality is the *expected signature* of any single limit cycle, and — importantly — **the peak locations read off the amplitude $A$ directly.**

This makes the following contradiction unavoidable.

> **Internal inconsistency (§IV-B).** The text states the pole "safely oscillates between two boundaries ($\approx\pm 0.5$ rad)" and, in the same paragraph, that "the maximum deviation remains strictly within the failure thresholds ($\pm 0.209$ rad)." Since $0.5>0.209$, these cannot both hold. By the density argument above, peaks at $\pm0.5$ rad imply $A\approx0.5$ rad, which would terminate every episode — contradicting the reported 100% success rate over 1000 steps. Either the figure's abscissa is not the pole angle (cart position is the plausible candidate), the units are not radians, or the $\pm0.5$ figure is erroneous. As published, the central stability result is not internally consistent and cannot be assessed.

### 2.7 Two contributors to the limit cycle, only one identified

The paper attributes the limit cycle entirely to action quantization. A sampled-data relay-like controller has a second, independent contribution. Outside a boundary layer around $\sigma=0$, sliding requires $\sigma\dot\sigma<0$; with sampling period $\Delta t$ the controller cannot react within a step, so the reachable boundary layer has thickness
$$\lvert\sigma\rvert\lesssim \Delta t\cdot\sup\lvert\dot\sigma\rvert,$$
giving a limit cycle of amplitude $O(\Delta t)$ and minimum period $2\Delta t$ — i.e. a chattering frequency capped at the Nyquist rate of the control loop. **$\Delta t$ is never reported.** Without it, the measured "Action Chattering Frequency" (§III-F) has no reference scale: a chattering frequency at the sampling limit means something very different from one well below it. Disentangling the quantization contribution $\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}(\varepsilon_\infty)$ from the sampling contribution $O(\Delta t)$ requires an ablation (vary tree depth at fixed $\Delta t$) that is not performed.

### 2.8 The "fundamental trade-off": quantified

The abstract promises a "fundamental trade-off" between continuous control and discrete interpretability but delivers only a qualitative observation. The trade-off is quantifiable, and doing so also refutes the paper's binary framing.

Let the tree induce partition $\{R_i\}_{i=1}^{L}$ with leaf outputs $c_i$, $L\le 2^{\text{depth}}$. Then
$$\sup_{s\in R_i}\lvert e_q(s)\rvert \;\ge\; \tfrac12\,\mathrm{osc}_{R_i}(\pi_T),\qquad \mathrm{osc}_{R_i}(\pi_T)=\sup_{R_i}\pi_T-\inf_{R_i}\pi_T,$$
with equality at the mid-range value; CART uses leaf *means*, so the inequality is generally strict. If $\pi_T$ has gradient magnitude at least $g>0$ over a region of volume $V$ in $d$ effective dimensions, an axis-aligned partition into $L$ cells forces some cell to have width $\gtrsim (V/L)^{1/d}$ along $\nabla\pi_T$, hence
$$\varepsilon_\infty \;=\; \Omega\!\Big(g\,V^{1/d}\,L^{-1/d}\Big) \;=\; \Omega\!\Big(g\,V^{1/d}\,2^{-\text{depth}/d}\Big).$$

Combining with §2.5, the ultimate bound (and therefore the limit-cycle amplitude) scales as
$$A \;\sim\; \gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}\big(\varepsilon_\infty\big)\;\sim\; 2^{-\text{depth}/d}\quad\text{(for locally linear }\gamma_{\mathrm{ISS}}\text{)}.$$

Equivalently, the depth needed for actuation error $\varepsilon$ is $\text{depth}\ \gtrsim\ d\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$. Three consequences:

1. **The trade-off is quantitative, not fundamental.** As $L\to\infty$, $\varepsilon_\infty\to0$ and (by continuity of $\pi_T$) the limit cycle vanishes. The claim that "replacing a smooth continuous gradient with discrete thresholds *inherently* induces Bang-Bang control" (§II-D) is false in the limit; bang-bang behavior at depth 7 is a *budget* artifact, not an inherent property of tree policies.
2. **Testable prediction the paper could have checked for free.** With $d\approx4$–$5$, each additional depth level should shrink the limit-cycle amplitude by only $2^{1/d}\approx1.15$–$1.19\times$. A depth sweep (3, 5, 7, 9, 11) plotting measured $A$ and chattering frequency against $2^{-\text{depth}/d}$ would convert the paper's qualitative observation into a validated scaling law. This is the paper's single largest missed opportunity.
3. **"Bang-Bang" is imprecise.** A depth-7 regressor emits up to 128 distinct force levels; bang-bang means two. The observed behavior is high-frequency switching among many levels, which is relay-*like* but not bang-bang. The term is used loosely throughout.

## 3. Theorem Analysis

**No theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries are stated.** For completeness, the two results the paper *relies on* implicitly:

### 3.1 Borrowed: VIPER's return-loss bound (Bastani et al. 2018) — cited by objective, never stated

- **Statement (informal).** For a finite action space, if DAgger-style aggregation with the $Q$-gap-weighted 0-1 loss achieves weighted error $\varepsilon$, the student's return loss relative to the teacher is bounded linearly in the horizon, $O(\varepsilon T)$, rather than the $O(\varepsilon T^2)$ of naive cloning.
- **Intuition.** Weighting by the suboptimality gap means the student is only penalized where a mistake actually costs value; states where all actions are near-equivalent are effectively free. This removes one factor of $T$ from error compounding.
- **Applicability here: weak.** The action space is continuous, the student is a regressor not a classifier, no aggregation loop is run, and no weighting is applied. The bound does not transfer, and the paper does not claim it does — but it also does not note that it doesn't.

### 3.2 Borrowed: sliding-mode existence conditions — not invoked at all

## 5. Review

### Theoretical Strengths

- Correctly identifies covariate shift as the central obstacle to naive behavioral cloning, and correctly points to the induced-distribution objective as the remedy.
- Correctly identifies action quantization as the mechanism linking discretization to actuation pathology, and names $e_q(s)=\pi_T(s)-\pi_S(s)$ as the right object to bound.

### Theoretical Limitations

1. **The stated method is not the implemented method.** Eqs. ## 4. Mathematical foundations

**Borrowed frameworks.** The paper sits on standard MDP theory [40] and inherits, without restating, the theoretical machinery of the five methods it benchmarks:

- *Density-based coverage* (MDPFuzz): a GMM $\hat{p}_\theta(s)$ fit over visited states; low $\hat{p}_\theta$ signals novelty. The theoretical premise is that likelihood under a fitted mixture is a usable surrogate for state-space coverage.
- *Prediction-error novelty* (CureFuzz): the Random Network Distillation argument that $\|f_{\text{pred}}(s) - f_{\text{target}}(s)\|^2$ approximates epistemic uncertainty, decaying with visitation count.
- *Distributional modeling* (G-Model): a diffusion model fit to nominal trajectories, then fine-tuned against a terminal-state-novelty reward — i.e. reward-guided sampling from the tails of a learned density.
- *Quality-Diversity search* (QDFuzz): MAP-Elites-style archive maintenance over a behavior-descriptor space, optimizing for an illuminated archive rather than a single optimum.
- *Learned early stopping* (SeqDivFuzz): a Siamese network estimates sequence diversity mid-episode to truncate low-value rollouts — implicitly an optimal-stopping / budget-allocation policy.

**Novel theoretical contributions.** None claimed and none present. The contributions are a unified benchmark, re-implementations, and empirical findings.

**A reconstruction the paper leaves implicit.** The paper's RQ1/RQ3 conclusion (throughput beats guidance) is presented as a bare empirical fact. It is straightforwardly explained by a budget decomposition the authors never write down. Under a fixed wall-clock budget $B$, with per-test cost $c_m$ for method $m$ (rollout cost plus guidance overhead) and per-test crash probability $p_m$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\#\text{UC}_m] \;\approx\; \underbrace{\frac{B}{c_m}}_{\text{throughput}} \cdot \; p_m \cdot \; \underbrace{(1 - \rho_m)}_{\text{non-redundancy}}$$

Guidance mechanisms raise $p_m$ and lower $\rho_m$ but also raise $c_m$ (GMM fitting, predictor updates, diffusion sampling). Whether a guided method wins depends entirely on the sign of $\partial \log p_m - \partial \log c_m$. This makes the paper's "inherent trade-off between crash quantity and crash diversity" claim precise, and it predicts *when* the finding should reverse: as base failure rates fall (harder, more robust agents), the $p_m$ term dominates and guidance should win. This is consistent with their own MountainCar-vs-CARLA contrast. The paper reports the pattern but does not offer the model, so its generalization claims rest on three data points rather than on an identified mechanism. I present this as my reconstruction, not as something derivable from the paper's text.

## 5. Review

### Theoretical strengths

- The MDP framing is correct and appropriate; nothing is misstated.
- Making the *validity* of generated test inputs a first-class methodological concern is a genuine conceptual advance over prior work that counts raw crashes. The differential-testing criterion is sound in the direction that matters (see below).
- Explicitly selecting robust agents (low base failure rate) to avoid ceiling effects is methodologically well-reasoned: if $p_{\text{base}}$ is high, all methods saturate and between-method variance is swamped.
- Including random testing as a stochastic lower bound is the right baseline discipline, and the paper is correct that its omission elsewhere is a real defect in the literature.

### Theoretical limitations

**(1) The unique-crash metric is ill-defined on continuous input spaces.** The paper defines two crashes as distinct if they differ in *either* the initial seed *or* the trajectory. Formally, let $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the seed and $\tau(x)$ the resulting trajectory; the stated equivalence is

$$(x_1, \tau_1) \sim (x_2, \tau_2) \iff x_1 = x_2 \;\wedge\; \tau_1 = \tau_2.$$

With $d = 15$ and $d = 63$ and continuous-valued mutation operators, $\Pr[x_1 = x_2] = 0$ for any two independently generated seeds. Every equivalence class is a singleton almost surely, so $\#\text{UC} = \#\text{crashes}$ identically. The metric therefore performs **no deduplication whatsoever** unless the paper specifies a quantization grid, a distance threshold $\epsilon$, or a trajectory abstraction. The excerpt is cut off precisely at the illustrative example ("an autonomous car may crash into the same obstacle from two slightly different initial positions, but the crashes stem from different model…"), which suggests the authors are aware of the issue and may resolve it. **This must be checked in the full text.** If unresolved, the primary effectiveness metric of the entire study reduces to raw crash count, which directly and trivially rewards throughput — making the RQ1 conclusion partly a definitional artifact rather than an empirical finding.

**(2) The validity criterion is sound but not complete, and the incompleteness is adversely correlated with severity.** Let $F(x)$ denote "config $x$ is feasible," $S_v(x)$ "validation policy succeeds on $x$," $S_a(x)$ "agent under test succeeds on $x$." The paper accepts $x$ iff $S_v(x) \wedge \neg S_a(x)$. Since $S_v(x) \Rightarrow F(x)$, no infeasible config is accepted: the filter is **sound**. But $F(x) \wedge \neg S_v(x)$ is possible, so feasible-but-hard configs are discarded: the filter is **incomplete**, and #UC is a conservative lower bound. The authors call this "conservative," which is accurate. The unaddressed problem is that the discarded set is exactly $\{x : F(x) \wedge \neg S_v(x)\}$ — the configs so difficult that even the strongest available policy fails. These are the most safety-relevant scenarios. The filter systematically removes the tail it is most important to characterize. With CaRL failing on 2.5% of random CARLA configs, and fuzzers deliberately searching toward hard regions, the discard rate on *fuzzer-generated* inputs should be far above 2.5% and is likely method-dependent.

**(3) Validity is policy-relative, which breaks cross-benchmark and cross-method comparability.** #UC is defined against a specific validation policy $\pi_v$ (PPO / SAC / CaRL). Different $\pi_v$ induce different accepted sets, so #UC values are not commensurable across benchmarks, and any future paper using a different $\pi_v$ cannot be compared to these numbers. This partially undercuts the paper's own stated goal of standardized evaluation. A policy-independent feasibility oracle, or an ensemble of validation policies with reported sensitivity, would be needed.

**(4) No uncertainty quantification on the reported failure rates.** With $n = 1000$ and $\hat{p} = 0.011$, the Wilson 95% interval is roughly $[0.006, 0.021]$; for $\hat{p} = 0.016$ it is roughly $[0.010, 0.026]$. The MountainCar and BipedalWalker rates are statistically indistinguishable. The paper treats these point estimates as precise characterizations of agent robustness. More broadly, the excerpt reports no repetition counts, random seeds, variance, or significance tests for any comparison — a serious gap for a study whose entire contribution is comparative claims about stochastic algorithms.

**(5) Reuse of the agent-selection set as the generalization test set.** §III-A states that the same 1,000 random configurations used to *select* the agents under test (by low failure rate) are "retained as an independent test set" to evaluate post-repair generalization and regression faults. This set is not independent of the selection procedure: agents were chosen because they perform well on it. The resulting generalization estimates are optimistically biased. The magnitude is probably small (selection was over few candidates on a coarse criterion), but the word "independent" is not justified.

**(6) The crash predicate is a hand-set threshold with no sensitivity analysis.** Crash conditions are step budgets and position thresholds (position $> 0.5$ within 200 steps; fall within 300 steps; collision or non-arrival within 200 steps). Each is a discretization of a continuous safety notion, and #UC is a discontinuous function of these constants. No sensitivity analysis is offered, so it is unknown how much of the between-method ordering survives a change from 200 to, say, 250 steps.

**(7) Cross-method safety-monitoring accuracy is reported without a base-rate argument.** The claim that monitors "typically exceed 95% accuracy even in cross-method settings" is uninterpretable without the class balance. If crash-triggering inputs are a small fraction of the evaluation set, 95% accuracy may be at or below the majority-class baseline. Precision/recall or AUC against a stated prior is required.

**(8) The RQ2 quantity–diversity trade-off is asserted, not established.** The paper calls it "an inherent trade-off." "Inherent" is a strong word implying a structural constraint. What is shown is a negative association across five methods on three benchmarks. No mechanism, no formal statement of the frontier, and no demonstration that a method cannot dominate on both axes. The budget decomposition in §4 above suggests the trade-off is a consequence of finite budget allocation rather than anything inherent — and that it is therefore avoidable by better allocation, or by simply spending more.

**(9) Diversity metrics absent from the available text.** RQ2 is one of four research questions, and the operational definition of diversity does not appear in the excerpt I have. Given that "diversity" is measured over at least two levels (input space and behavior space) by different methods with different internal notions of it (RND novelty vs. QD behavior descriptors vs. Siamese sequence distance), the choice of a *neutral* diversity metric is the single most consequential methodological decision in RQ2. Any metric that coincides with one method's own objective will favor that method by construction. This needs verification in the full paper.

### Questions for further investigation

1. Does the full §III-D define an $\epsilon$-threshold or abstraction for crash uniqueness? If not, does any RQ1 conclusion survive replacing #UC with a properly quantized count?
2. What fraction of each method's generated crashes is discarded by the validity filter, and does that fraction differ significantly across methods? A method whose crashes are disproportionately filtered is being penalized for finding hard scenarios.
3. Is the RQ2 diversity metric independent of all five methods' internal novelty objectives? Which method's objective does it most resemble?
4. How many repetitions per (method, benchmark) cell, and what is the between-seed variance of #UC? Are the reported method rankings stable under reseeding?
5. What is the class balance in the safety-monitoring experiments, and what is the majority-class baseline?
6. Does the throughput advantage of MDPFuzz persist under an iteration-matched budget rather than a time-matched one? Reporting both would separate the $c_m$ effect from the $p_m$ effect in the decomposition above.
7. The paper recommends "combining complementary fuzzing strategies." Is there evidence that the union of two methods' crash sets exceeds either alone by more than the increase in budget would predict, i.e. genuine complementarity rather than additivity?
8. Under what agent-robustness regime does the throughput-over-guidance conclusion reverse? Only three base failure rates (1.1%, 1.6%, 7.2%) are sampled, and the two low ones are statistically indistinguishable, so the paper effectively has two points on this axis.

# Theoretical Analysis: *What do Reward Models Memorize?* (arXiv 2607.24484)

## 0. Verdict up front

This is an empirical paper with a thin formal layer: it contains **no theorems, no lemmas, and no proofs**. Its theoretical content consists of (i) one borrowed probabilistic model (Bradley–Terry), (ii) one borrowed estimand (counterfactual memorization) instantiated in a new domain, (iii) two undefined-in-body summary statistics over Shapley values, and (iv) a supervised sparse-autoencoder objective. The interesting theoretical work is therefore not in what is proved but in **what the estimand actually measures**, and here there are two structural problems that plausibly generate two of the paper's three headline findings as artifacts. I develop these in §5.2.

Eq. (2) as printed is not well-formed. Written as

$$\mathrm{CM} = \mathbb{E}[p(y^+\succ y^-)\mid y^+,y^-\in\mathcal{D}^{(\mathrm{train})}] - \mathbb{E}[p(y^+\succ y^-)\mid y^+,y^-\notin\mathcal{D}^{(\mathrm{train})}]$$

it (a) omits the model parameters, on which $p$ entirely depends; (b) leaves the measure of $\mathbb{E}$ unspecified — it is over the random draw of the training subset and the training randomness, not over data; (c) conditions on membership of the *responses* rather than of the *pair*; and (d) suppresses the index $i$, so it reads as a dataset-level scalar when it is a per-pair quantity. The intended object is presumably

$$\mathrm{CM}(i) = \mathbb{E}_{S\,:\,i\in S}\Big[\sigma\big(\mathrm{rm}_{\theta(S)}(y_i^+\mid x_i) - \mathrm{rm}_{\theta(S)}(y_i^-\mid x_i)\big)\Big] \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{S\,:\,i\notin S}\Big[\sigma\big(\cdot\big)\Big]$$

where $S$ is a sampled user-level subset and $\theta(S)$ the resulting LoRA parameters. This reconstruction matters, because writing it out exposes the estimand mismatch in §5.2/G1.

## 2. Key Derivations

There are no multi-step derivations in the paper. What follows are the four inferential chains that do the argumentative work, reconstructed and checked.

### 2.1 Derivation A — from BT loss to $\mathrm{TM}$

**Starting point.** Eq. (1): $p(y^+\succ y^-)=\sigma(\Delta)$ with $\Delta := \mathrm{rm}(y^+\mid x)-\mathrm{rm}(y^-\mid x)$.

**Step 1.** Discriminative RM training maximizes the BT log-likelihood, i.e. minimizes $\ell_i = -\log\sigma(\Delta_i)$ per pair.

**Step 2.** Hence $p_i = \exp(-\ell_i)$, so $\mathrm{TM}(i) = \mathbb{E}_{S:i\in S}[\exp(-\ell_i)]$.

**Result.** $\mathrm{TM}$ is a monotone transform of the *per-example training loss*, and $\mathrm{TG}$ of the *per-example held-out loss*. Therefore

$$\mathrm{CM}(i) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[e^{-\ell_i^{\text{train}}}] - \mathbb{E}[e^{-\ell_i^{\text{held-out}}}]$$

is **exactly an instance-level train/test generalization gap under an exponential-of-negative-loss transform**.

**Interpretation.** This is a legitimate operationalization of Feldman-style memorization, but it is worth stating plainly that nothing in the construction distinguishes "memorization" from "instance-level overfitting"; the memorization reading is an interpretation layered on a generalization-gap statistic. The paper never says this.

**One consequence the paper misses.** Two identities matter:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial \Delta} = \sigma(\Delta)\big(1-\sigma(\Delta)\big), \qquad \max_\Delta \frac{\partial p}{\partial\Delta} = \tfrac14 \text{ at } \Delta = 0.$$

The probability scale is *compressive*: for $|\Delta|\gtrsim 5$ the derivative is below $0.007$. So $\mathrm{CM}$ measured in probability space systematically **understates** memorization exactly for the pairs where the reward margin moved most. Since the paper reports that the mass of pairs sits near $(1,1)$, most of its data lie in the saturated regime, and the "graded definition of instance-level memorization" that motivates the whole approach is degraded to a near-binary one. A logit-space alternative,

$$\mathrm{CM}^{\text{logit}}(i) = \mathbb{E}_{S:i\in S}[\Delta_i] - \mathbb{E}_{S:i\notin S}[\Delta_i],$$

is unbounded, avoids ceiling effects, and — a useful property, since BT identifies $\mathrm{rm}$ only up to a per-prompt additive shift — is invariant to that non-identifiability because it depends only on the difference $\Delta$. I see no reason this was not used and no discussion of the choice.

### 2.2 Derivation B — the ceiling constraint on $\mathrm{CM}$

Not in the paper; it follows immediately and undercuts one reported finding.

Since $\mathrm{TM},\mathrm{TG}\in(0,1)$,

$$\mathrm{CM}(i) = \mathrm{TM}(i)-\mathrm{TG}(i) \;\le\; 1-\mathrm{TG}(i).$$

The paper reports (§3) that "most preference pairs have high $\mathrm{TG}$ scores, and preference pairs with high $\mathrm{CM}$ are rare, resulting in a dense cluster of preference pairs near coordinate $(1,1)$." The second clause is **not independent evidence** of anything: it is entailed by the first. High $\mathrm{TG}$ mechanically caps $\mathrm{CM}$. Presenting "high CM is rare" as an observation alongside "TG is high" double-counts a single fact.

The same constraint biases the downstream attribution analysis: conditional on $\mathrm{TM}$ being near its ceiling, $\mathrm{CM}\approx 1-\mathrm{TG}$, so the $\mathrm{CM}$ regressor is largely re-estimating the $\mathrm{TG}$ regressor with a sign flip. The reported $R^2$ values ($\mathrm{TM}$: 0.56/0.63) show $\mathrm{TM}$ retains real variance, so I cannot claim this degeneracy is total — but the paper provides no diagnostic (e.g. $\mathrm{corr}(\mathrm{CM},-\mathrm{TG})$, or the marginal variance of $\mathrm{TM}$) to bound how close to it we are. Without that number, every "$\mathrm{CM}$ is high for feature $X$" claim in §4 is ambiguous between "$X$ requires memorization" and "$X$ is simply hard to classify held-out."

### 2.3 Derivation C — variance of the estimator

The paper trains 25 RMs, each pair appearing in 20 subsets and held out in 5. Let $s$ be the across-run standard deviation of $p_i$. Then

$$\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{TM}}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{20}} \approx 0.224\,s, \quad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{TG}}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{5}} \approx 0.447\,s,$$
$$\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{CM}}) = s\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{20}+\tfrac{1}{5}} = 0.5\,s.$$

The held-out arm carries **4× the variance** of the training arm, and $\mathrm{CM}$'s noise is dominated by it. For a plausible $s\approx0.2$, $\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{CM}})\approx 0.1$ — half the width of the $\mathrm{CM}$ bins used to color Figure 2. Two implications:

1. The paper's attribution of negative-$\mathrm{CM}$ pairs to "noise" (§3) is almost certainly right, but it is asserted rather than quantified. With 25 runs in hand, a per-pair standard error was directly available and is not reported (in the visible text).
2. The fine structure of the memorization map — and any claim that a particular region is sparse or dense in a particular $\mathrm{CM}$ bin — may not be resolvable at this noise level. The 4-region discretization in §5 inherits this.

A 20/5 allocation is also an odd choice under a decision-theoretic view. If the goal is minimum-variance $\widehat{\mathrm{CM}}$ with 25 runs and roughly equal $s$ in both arms, the optimal allocation is balanced (12/13), giving $\mathrm{SE}\approx0.283\,s$ — a **43% reduction** over the chosen design at identical compute. The 20/5 split makes sense only if 80%-of-users training subsets were required for the RMs to be adequately trained, which is plausible but never argued.

### 2.4 Derivation D — checking the footnote-2 argument

The paper claims: "Features which are equally important to training memorization and testing generalization have no explanatory power on counterfactual memorization, which explains the reduced $R^2$-score."

**Check.** In a linear-additive setting, write $\mathrm{TM}=\beta_M^\top z+\varepsilon_M$ and $\mathrm{TG}=\beta_G^\top z+\varepsilon_G$. Then

$$\mathrm{CM}=(\beta_M-\beta_G)^\top z + (\varepsilon_M-\varepsilon_G), \qquad R^2_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}\big((\beta_M-\beta_G)^\top z\big)}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{CM})}.$$

If $\beta_M=\beta_G$ the numerator vanishes and $R^2_{\mathrm{CM}}=0$. **The argument is correct as far as it goes.**

**But it is incomplete in a way that matters.** The denominator also grows: $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_M-\varepsilon_G)=\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_M)+\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_G)-2\mathrm{Cov}$, and by §2.3 the $\varepsilon_G$ term is the largest of the three. So $R^2_{\mathrm{CM}}<R^2_{\mathrm{TG}}$ is predicted by **estimator noise alone**, with no signal cancellation required. The observed pattern is consistent with both channels ($0.39<0.52$ for PRISM, $0.33<0.38$ for COMMUNITY), and the paper attributes it entirely to cancellation without ruling out noise.

These are distinguishable. Split the 20 in-subsets into two halves of 10, compute split-half reliability $\rho$ for $\widehat{\mathrm{TM}}$ (and analogously for $\widehat{\mathrm{TG}}$ with 2/3 or 3/2), and use $\rho$ as the attainable $R^2$ ceiling. Reporting $R^2/\rho$ instead of raw $R^2$ would settle it. As presented, "explains the reduced $R^2$" is an unfalsified plausibility claim.

**A second, sharper problem with the same footnote.** The paper's inferential rule — "large positive $\mathrm{CM}$ features indicate memorization … large negative $\mathrm{CM}$ features indicate overgeneralization" — implicitly relies on $\varphi^{\mathrm{CM}} = \varphi^{\mathrm{TM}} - \varphi^{\mathrm{TG}}$. Shapley values satisfy local accuracy per model, $\sum_d \varphi_{n,d} = g(z_n) - \mathbb{E}[g]$, but three *separately fit nonlinear* MLPs $g_{\mathrm{TM}}, g_{\mathrm{TG}}, g_{\mathrm{CM}}$ do not satisfy $g_{\mathrm{CM}} = g_{\mathrm{TM}} - g_{\mathrm{TG}}$ pointwise, so their Shapley values do not obey the subtraction identity either. The exact target-level identity $\mathrm{CM}=\mathrm{TM}-\mathrm{TG}$ does **not** transfer to the fitted predictions. A clean fix exists and is cheap: fit a single multi-output regressor with a hard architectural constraint $g_{\mathrm{CM}} := g_{\mathrm{TM}} - g_{\mathrm{TG}}$, under which the identity holds exactly by SHAP's linearity in the model output. The paper does not do this and does not note the gap.

## 3. Theorem Analysis

**There are no theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries in this paper.** For completeness I record the three load-bearing informal claims and evaluate them as if they were propositions, since that is where the theoretical scrutiny belongs.

### Claim 1 — "RMs misallocate memorization to easy, high-margin preference pairs"

**Statement.** User-preference-margin features dominate $\mathrm{TM}$ but predict $\mathrm{TG}$ far worse, yielding high $\mathrm{CM}$; the RM "learns to recognize easy examples in its training data and maximizes the RM margin on these" but cannot recognize them held out. This is called "a misallocation of memorization capacity."

**Intuition.** Memorization budget is being spent where it is least needed, instead of on the long tail where Feldman's theory says it is necessary.

**Evidence structure.** SHAP on margin features, high for $\mathrm{TM}$, low for $\mathrm{TG}$, in both datasets.

**Assessment — partially tautological, and the key word is unformalized.**

*(a) For PRISM the finding is close to circular.* The paper states the margin "was included in the RM training objective." If the loss is a margin-BT variant, $\ell_i = -\log\sigma(\Delta_i - m_i)$, then the optimizer is *explicitly instructed* to drive $\Delta_i$ above $m_i$ on training pairs. Measuring $\mathrm{TM}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\Delta_i)]$ and finding it increases in $m_i$ is close to reporting that the optimizer minimized its loss. The paper recognizes this ("not altogether surprising") and shifts the weight of the claim onto COMMUNITY, which is the right move.

*(b) For COMMUNITY the margin is coarser than described.* The paper says the COMMUNITY margin is "implicit and granular." From the reported counts I can check this: $45.8\times10^3$ turns $\times\,3 = 137.4\times10^3 \approx 137\times10^3$ reported pairs. With 4 responses per turn, all-pairs would give $\binom{4}{2}=6$ per turn ($\approx 275\times10^3$). So COMMUNITY is almost certainly constructed as **chosen-vs-each-of-three-others**, making the implicit margin the *rank of the rejected response* — a **3-level ordinal variable**, not a granular one. (Derived arithmetic, not stated in the paper; worth confirming against Appendix A.) The same check on PRISM ($8.0\times10^3$ turn-1s at $\approx4.6$ pairs each $+\;19.2\times10^3$ later turns $\times 1 \approx 56\times10^3$ vs. $57.9\times10^3$ reported) is consistent with all-pairs construction, so the two datasets differ in pairing scheme — a difference that is never mentioned but affects every cross-dataset comparison in §4.

*(c) "Misallocation of capacity" is asserted, not shown.* A misallocation claim requires a budget and a demonstrated trade-off: total memorization is conserved, so spending it on easy pairs crowds out hard ones. Neither exists here. LoRA actually offers a natural formalization — rank $r$ bounds the adaptation subspace, so a capacity argument is available — but the paper does not construct it. What would be needed is an intervention: vary $r$ (or margin weighting), and show that reducing high-margin $\mathrm{CM}$ *increases* low-margin $\mathrm{CM}$. Absent that, the observation is "memorization correlates with margin," which is much weaker than "misallocation." Note also that Feldman's result — memorization of rare subpopulations is necessary for near-optimal generalization under long-tailed data — does **not** imply that memorizing easy examples is harmful. The rhetorical bridge from Feldman to "misallocation" does not carry weight.

### Claim 2 — "RMs memorize dataset-specific shortcuts (model identity, user sampling strategy)"

**Statement.** For PRISM, response-model identity is the strongest $\mathrm{CM}$ determinant; for COMMUNITY, user-level features rank second, driven by one education group that came almost entirely from a second recruitment wave.

**Assessment — the model-identity half is confounded; the user half is largely produced by the estimator design.**

*(a) Model identity is entangled with turn position.* Per §3, PRISM turn 1 receives $\approx3.75$ responses from 21 different models, while later turns receive 2 responses from **the same model**. So model-identity variation exists *only* in turn 1. Turn-1 pairs are $\approx 64\%$ of PRISM pairs (derived above). Any "model identity" effect is therefore inseparable from "first turn vs. later turn," which differ in prompt distribution and in the presence of conversational history. Figure 4 includes a "Not Turn 1" series, which suggests the authors saw the structure, but they interpret it as the *same-model* condition rather than as the *turn-position* condition it also is. A within-turn-1 analysis is needed and is not reported.

*(b) The user-trait finding is close to guaranteed by the splitting scheme.* This is the most serious issue in the paper. Because subsets are split **at the user level**, the counterfactual event is not "pair $i$ withheld" but "the user $u(i)$ and all their data withheld." The estimand is therefore a *group* counterfactual, which decomposes as

$$\mathrm{CM}^{\text{group}}(i) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{CM}^{\text{self}}(i)}_{\text{Feldman's quantity}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{j\neq i,\; u(j)=u(i)} \mathrm{Infl}(j \to i)}_{\text{same-user spillover}}.$$

Pairs per user: PRISM $57.9\times10^3/1.40\times10^3 \approx 41$; COMMUNITY $137\times10^3/0.77\times10^3 \approx 178$. The spillover sum runs over $\approx40$ and $\approx177$ terms respectively, so unless same-user influence is near zero the spillover term **dominates**. And COMMUNITY — the dataset with $4\times$ the pairs per user — is precisely where the "User" block jumps to 2nd-highest $\mathrm{CM}$ MAV. That ordering is what the artifact predicts.

The user-level split is the right choice for avoiding preference leakage; the mistake is calling the resulting quantity counterfactual memorization and interpreting it as *the RM memorizing a user trait*. What is measured is closer to **counterfactual user influence**. To recover the intended pair-level estimand one needs nested splits (hold out the user's *other* pairs while retaining pair $i$, and vice versa) to separate $\mathrm{CM}^{\text{self}}$ from spillover. Appendix B reportedly addresses "user–user confounding," which is a related but distinct issue (confounding *between* users, not self-vs-spillover *within* a user); I cannot rule out that Appendix B handles this, and this critique should be checked against it before being treated as settled.

*(c) The mediation claim rests on two null results.* The conclusion that the effect is "mediated through their prompts and preferences" follows from finding no group difference in English proficiency or topic distribution. Two absent differences on measured covariates is weak support for a mediation claim, the nulls are likely underpowered given the group size, and the recruitment-wave explanation the authors themselves surface introduces an unaddressed *temporal* confound (wave-2 users may have interacted with different model versions or in a different period). A group-size-matched comparison against other user groups is the missing control.

### Claim 3 — "RMs overgeneralize simple heuristic correlates (length, compliance)"

**Statement.** Most annotations have larger MAV for $\mathrm{TG}$ than $\mathrm{TM}$, driving $\mathrm{CM}$ negative; the RM relies on these features held out but attenuates them when the pair was seen. Concretely: complexity/length differences aligned with the heuristic net negative $\mathrm{CM}$ SHAP; violations net positive.

**Assessment — the observation is real, but a benign alternative explanation is equally consistent and is not excluded.**

The paper's reading: the RM over-applies a shortcut to unseen data. A variance-decomposition reading is just as consistent: on **training** pairs the RM has additional example-specific information available (it memorized them), so any single global heuristic explains a *smaller share* of its behavior; on **held-out** pairs only the heuristic is available, so the heuristic's share is *larger* by construction. Under this reading, $\mathrm{MAV}_{\mathrm{TG}}>\mathrm{MAV}_{\mathrm{TM}}$ for nearly all features is the *expected* signature of any model that memorizes anything at all — and the paper does report that it holds for "the majority of our annotations," which is what the benign account predicts and the harmful account does not specifically predict.

Distinguishing them requires an intervention on the feature rather than an attribution over an observational surrogate: construct length-controlled or compliance-controlled counterfactual pairs and measure $\Delta\mathrm{rm}$ directly. That test is clean, falsifiable, cheap, and absent. As it stands, the strongest defensible version of Claim 3 is: *held-out RM behavior is more predictable from surface heuristics than training behavior is* — which is true and interesting, but is not yet evidence of harmful overgeneralization.

There is also an internal inconsistency in how the claim is stated. §4.3 defines the memorization/overgeneralization test via **signed $\varphi^{\mathrm{CM}}$**, while the "Heuristics" paragraph runs the test via **MAV comparisons between the $\mathrm{TM}$ and $\mathrm{TG}$ regressors**. These are different quantities: MAV is a magnitude and discards sign, so $\mathrm{MAV}_{\mathrm{TG}}>\mathrm{MAV}_{\mathrm{TM}}$ does not imply $\varphi^{\mathrm{CM}}<0$ (a feature can matter more for $\mathrm{TG}$ with the *opposite* sign). The two criteria are used interchangeably in the text.

## 4. Mathematical Foundations

### 4.1 Underlying frameworks

**Bradley–Terry / Luce choice theory.** Eq. (1) assumes a single scalar latent utility, stochastic transitivity, and rater homogeneity. The paper's own §5 finding — a medium/medium region characterized by "cases where multiple responses might be preferred, depending on the stance of the user," i.e. user–user preference conflict — is direct evidence of BT **misspecification**, not merely of a hard subpopulation. Under heterogeneous rater utilities, aggregate pairwise preference need not be transitive (Condorcet), so no scalar $\mathrm{rm}$ can represent it and the MLE converges to a projection onto the BT family. The paper's central finding, that the RM memorizes user identity and user traits, is then *what a correctly-optimized misspecified model must do*: absorb hidden rater context into example-specific parameters, because it cannot represent it structurally.

This is the paper's largest missed opportunity. From recall (flagged as unverified: I could not check these references), there is directly relevant theory here — Siththaranjan et al. on preference learning under hidden context, which shows BT-based RLHF implicitly performs a Borda-count-style aggregation over heterogeneous utilities, and the distributional-preference-learning line that follows from it. Connecting $\mathrm{CM}$ on user-level features to a hidden-context misspecification argument would convert three empirical observations into one explanation with a formal basis. The paper cites neither.

**Feldman-style counterfactual memorization.** Correctly borrowed in spirit, but altered in three ways that break comparability with prior work, none of which is discussed: the correctness signal is a **probability** rather than 0-1 accuracy (so $\mathrm{CM}$ absorbs confidence sharpening — a pair going from $p=0.6$ to $p=0.99$ registers as memorization with no change in the decision); inclusion probability is **80%** rather than the $\sim$50%/leave-one-out conventions; and the counterfactual is at the **group** (user) level rather than the instance level (§3, Claim 2b).

**Shapley attribution.** Two layers of concern. First, the surrogate layer: SHAP explains the MLP regressor, not the RM. All feature claims are valid only to the extent the surrogate fits — and it fits at $R^2 = 0.33$ for COMMUNITY $\mathrm{CM}$, leaving two-thirds of the variance unexplained. The paper's framing ("RM performance is largely, and in some cases mostly, explained by a relatively small set of features") is generous to $R^2=0.33$. Second, identifiability: with 84 heavily correlated annotation features (length, complexity, dialog acts, and emotion are not independent), Shapley credit is distributed across correlated features and per-feature attributions are not identifiable as effects of any one feature. The paper says it estimates **conditional** Shapley values; for an MLP with a 1,000-sample reference set, standard KernelSHAP computes the **interventional/marginal** variant unless a conditional-expectation model was explicitly fit. The distinction is not cosmetic — conditional and interventional Shapley disagree exactly under the feature correlation present here, and the conditional variant assigns nonzero credit to features the model never reads. Which was actually computed needs to be settled in Appendix F.

**Sparse dictionary learning.** See §4.3 below; this is where the most avoidable technical problem sits.

### 4.2 The novel summary statistics (MAV, HMR) — two measurement artifacts

**HMR over-weights rare top-1 events.** HMR is stated to be the harmonic mean of block ranks, i.e. $\mathrm{HMR}=1/\mathrm{MRR}$ with $\mathrm{MRR}=\frac1N\sum_n 1/\mathrm{rank}_n$. This is arithmetically fine, but harmonic means are dominated by their smallest terms. Concretely: a block ranked 1st in 5% of rows and 80th in the other 95% gets $\mathrm{MRR}=0.05+0.95/80=0.0619$, so $\mathrm{HMR}=16.2$ — appearing *far* more important than a block ranked a consistent 60th ($\mathrm{HMR}=60$), despite being 80th almost always. Since HMR is one of only two summary metrics carrying the §4 argument, and Figures 3 and 5 report HMR values spanning 1.7 to 57, some of the apparent importance orderings may be driven by rare top-1 events rather than typical behavior. A median rank or a rank distribution would be robust; neither is shown.

**Block-level net SHAP is not comparable across blocks of different size.** Block sizes are anthropomorphism 1, complexity 9, dialog acts 23, emotion 28, politeness 4, rule-based 16, sycophancy 3 (sum $=84$ ✓, matches the stated total). If "net" means the signed sum within a block, then for roughly independent signs $|{\sum}|$ grows like $\sqrt{k}$, so a 28-feature block is favored over a 1-feature block by a factor of $\approx 5$ **before any substantive difference**. Both figures place AnthroScore ($k=1$) last in MAV in both datasets and rank Emotions ($k=28$), Rule-based ($k=16$), and Dialog Acts ($k=23$) high — consistent with a size artifact. Signed summation within a block also cancels opposing within-block effects, so the direction of the net bias is not fully determined, but non-comparability across blocks stands. Size-normalized MAV (e.g. per-feature mean, or $|{\sum}|/\sqrt{k}$) is the obvious control and is not reported.

To be fair to the authors, one comparison runs *against* the size artifact and is therefore strengthened by it: PRISM's User block has 77 metadata features and ranks only mid-MAV, while COMMUNITY's User block has 28 and ranks 2nd. That contrast cannot be a block-size effect. (Feature counts derived from the stated metadata levels: PRISM $77{+}10{+}1{+}3=91$ metadata $+\,84$ annotations $=175$ exogenous features; COMMUNITY $28+84=112$.)

**Multiplicity.** Roughly 175 (or 112) features × 3 targets × 2 datasets, plus block aggregates and the Figure 5 feature-flip contrasts. Bonferroni correction is applied only to the SAE feature descriptions in §5. No correction, confidence intervals, or across-seed variability is reported for any SHAP-based claim, despite 25 trained RMs making a bootstrap directly available.

### 4.3 The SAE construction — a misspecification and a circularity

**Misspecification: nonnegative sparse codes on a signed difference space.** The SAE is trained on $f(x,y^+)-f(x,y^-)$. The superposition hypothesis that licenses SAE interpretation posits that *activations* are approximately nonnegative sparse combinations of dictionary atoms. A **difference** of two such combinations has **signed** coefficients. A TopK/ReLU dictionary with nonnegative codes therefore cannot represent negation and must learn *separate* atoms for $+d$ and $-d$, splitting an already tiny dictionary. With a stated maximum of 32 latents and $L_0\approx4$, this is severe: the effective dictionary for oriented features may be $\approx16$. The natural fix is an antisymmetry constraint — the representation should satisfy $z(y^+,y^-) = -z(y^-,y^+)$ under swap, e.g. via a signed code or a tied $\pm$ atom pair — since the underlying preference problem *is* antisymmetric. Neither the constraint nor the issue is mentioned. (Movva et al. face the same issue; inheriting it from the source work does not resolve it.)

**Circularity: the auxiliary loss creates the association it then reports.** The objective is L2 reconstruction **plus an auxiliary $\mathrm{CM}$-region classification loss on the sparse latents**; the region-classifier weights are then "interpreted as associations between a region and a feature." But the latents were *optimized to be region-discriminative*. Reporting that they discriminate regions restates the training objective. This is a supervised probe, not unsupervised feature discovery, and it should be described as such. Note also that the reported significance testing ($p<0.05$, Bonferroni) validates the **LLM-generated description against the latent**, not the **latent-to-region association**; the association carries no error control at all. The paper's own hedging ("tentatively suggest," "substantially more difficult to find feature differentiation") is appropriate, and to their credit the differential result — clear differentiation in PRISM, weak in COMMUNITY — is *not* what pure circularity alone would produce, since the objective was identical across datasets. That asymmetry is the strongest thing in §5.

**The discretization is weakest where the claim is strongest.** Four disjoint regions replace the continuous $\mathrm{CM}$, with unspecified boundaries. By the paper's own §3 finding, the high-$\mathrm{TM}$/low-$\mathrm{TG}$ (high-$\mathrm{CM}$) region is the **sparsest**, so its classifier is fit on the fewest examples — and it is precisely the region whose interpretation (brief, single-word chosen responses violating the length heuristic) carries the §5 argument. Combined with $\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{CM}})\approx0.1$ from §2.3, region assignment near boundaries is itself noisy. No region counts, boundary values, or classifier accuracies are reported in the visible text.

### 4.4 What is genuinely novel

Stated fairly, the theoretical contributions are: (i) the first instantiation of counterfactual memorization for reward models, i.e. defining the estimand over BT pairwise probabilities; (ii) two block-level SHAP summary statistics (MAV, HMR); (iii) the feature-flip contrast in Figure 5, which is a sensible paired-difference design and the most convincing estimator in the paper because it holds the pair fixed and varies only feature orientation; (iv) adding a region-supervision term to a preference-difference SAE. None of these is a mathematical result. The paper's value is empirical and taxonomic, and the abstract does not overclaim otherwise.

## 5. Review

### 5.1 Theoretical strengths

- **Correct core estimand, cleanly connected to loss.** $\mathrm{CM} = \mathrm{TM}-\mathrm{TG}$ under a BT-probability correctness signal is a defensible instantiation of Feldman/Zhang memorization, and it reduces exactly to an instance-level generalization gap (§2.1) — an unusually interpretable target.
- **Map geometry is right.** $\nabla\mathrm{CM}=(1,-1)$ is indeed perpendicular to the $\mathrm{TM}=\mathrm{TG}$ diagonal, with maximum at $(1,0)$; the presentation is internally consistent.
- **The resampling design is the expensive, correct choice.** 25 independently trained RMs with user-level splits is real compute spent on the right thing; most work in this area substitutes a single-model heuristic. Splitting at the user level to block preference leakage is the right instinct even though it changes the estimand.
- **Two-dataset replication with a genuine dissociation.** Findings that hold in both (margin) are separated from findings that hold in one (model identity in PRISM, user traits in COMMUNITY), and the paper explains the dissociation structurally (same-model responses in COMMUNITY). This is stronger design than a single-dataset study.
- **The feature-flip contrast (Fig. 5) is well constructed.** Conditioning on a within-pair orientation swap holds the pair fixed and isolates the feature's contribution — much better identified than the marginal SHAP summaries.
- **Appropriate hedging where warranted.** §5's "tentatively suggest," the explicit footnote about COMMUNITY's rare feature differences, the acknowledgment that PRISM's margin was in the objective, and the §6 concession that memorization is not inherently bad all reflect honest reporting.

### 5.2 Theoretical limitations

**G1 — The estimand is a group counterfactual, not an instance one, and this likely manufactures the user-trait finding.** User-level splitting makes the withheld unit the user, not the pair, so $\mathrm{CM}^{\text{group}} = \mathrm{CM}^{\text{self}} + \sum_{j: u(j)=u(i)} \mathrm{Infl}(j\to i)$ over $\approx40$ (PRISM) / $\approx177$ (COMMUNITY) same-user terms. That COMMUNITY has $4\times$ the pairs per user *and* is the dataset where the User block jumps to 2nd in $\mathrm{CM}$ MAV is exactly the artifact's prediction. Nested splits are needed to separate the two components. **Most consequential issue in the paper**, conditional on Appendix B not already handling it.

**G2 — Ceiling effects make $\mathrm{CM}$ partly redundant with $-\mathrm{TG}$.** $\mathrm{CM}\le 1-\mathrm{TG}$, so "high $\mathrm{CM}$ is rare" is entailed by "$\mathrm{TG}$ is high" rather than being separate evidence, and $\mathrm{CM}$-based feature claims are ambiguous between "requires memorization" and "hard held-out." Compounded by $\partial\sigma/\partial\Delta \to 0$ in the saturated regime where most of the paper's data live, which destroys the gradedness that motivated the metric. A logit-space $\mathrm{CM}$ fixes both and is shift-invariant under BT's non-identifiability (§2.1).

**G8 — BT misspecification is observed but not theorized.** The paper's medium/medium region *is* rater heterogeneity, which makes a scalar BT reward misspecified and can render aggregate preference intransitive. ## 6. 总体总结（中文）

本文是一篇实证研究，**不含任何定理、引理或证明**，其理论层面仅由 Bradley–Terry 模型、Feldman/Zhang 式反事实记忆（CM）估计量、两个新提出的 SHAP 汇总统计量（MAV、HMR），以及一个带监督辅助损失的稀疏自编码器目标构成。理论审查的重点因此不在证明是否成立，而在**该估计量究竟测量了什么**。

经推导可确认三点是正确的：$\mathrm{CM}=\mathrm{TM}-\mathrm{TG}$ 恰好等价于「样本级训练/测试泛化差距」的单调变换；记忆图的几何（梯度方向 $(1,-1)$、最大值在 $(1,0)$）无误；脚注 2 关于 $R^2_{\mathrm{CM}}$ 偏低的线性论证在信号抵消这一路径上成立。

但存在两个结构性缺陷，很可能直接**制造**了论文三大发现中的两个。第一，由于按**用户级**划分子集，反事实事件是「移除该用户全部数据」而非「移除该样本对」，估计量实为群体反事实：$\mathrm{CM}^{\text{group}}=\mathrm{CM}^{\text{self}}+\sum_{u(j)=u(i)}\mathrm{Infl}(j\to i)$。按其自报数据，PRISM 每用户约 41 对、COMMUNITY 约 178 对，溢出项极可能主导；而「用户特征」恰恰在每用户样本数为四倍的 COMMUNITY 中跃升为 CM 第二重要块——这正是该伪影的预测结果。第二，$\mathrm{CM}\le 1-\mathrm{TG}$ 的天花板约束使「高 CM 罕见」由「TG 普遍偏高」直接推出，并非独立证据；叠加 $\sigma$ 在饱和区导数趋零，恰好破坏了该指标赖以立论的「分级性」。改用 logit 空间的 $\mathrm{CM}^{\text{logit}}$ 可同时解决二者，且在 BT 的可识别性意义下具平移不变性。

其余主要问题包括：估计量噪声未量化且 20/5 分配次优（改为均衡分配可在同等算力下降低 43% 标准误）；三个独立拟合的非线性回归器的 SHAP 值被当作可相减使用；块级 net SHAP 随块规模按 $\sqrt{k}$ 增长而块规模跨度为 1 至 28；HMR 作为秩的调和平均会被少量 top-1 事件主导；SAE 在有符号的嵌入差空间上使用非负稀疏码属模型误设，且用带区域分类辅助损失训练出的隐变量再去解释区域关联存在循环论证；模型身份效应在 PRISM 中与轮次位置不可分（模型差异仅存在于第 1 轮，占约 64% 样本对）；「记忆容量错配」缺少预算定义与挤出效应的实证。最关键的是，从记忆化捷径到 reward hacking 的因果桥梁**始终未被建立**，而这正是全文动机与结论的支点。

最大的理论遗憾在于：论文自己观察到的「用户间偏好冲突」区域本身即为 BT 的模型误设证据；在隐藏上下文（hidden context）视角下，「RM 记忆用户身份」恰是一个被正确优化的误设标量模型必然产生的行为。将三个发现统一到这一机制之下，本可把三项经验观察转化为一个有理论依据的解释。

**总评**：现象学层面的贡献真实且有价值（两数据集、25 个独立训练的 RM、可复现的特征标注体系），但估计量与其宣称含义之间的落差足以动摇其中两项主张。建议的最小修复顺序为：嵌套划分以分离自身效应与用户溢出 → logit 空间 CM → 报告每样本对标准误与分半信度 → 限定于 PRISM 第 1 轮重估模型身份效应。

## 2. Key Derivations

### 2.1 From GRPO to ACRL (Eqs. (1) → (2) → (3) → (10))

**Starting point.** GRPO, Eq. (1): expectation over $a_i\sim\mu$, ratio built from $\pi$. The paper correctly identifies the inconsistency: the estimator is an on-policy estimator evaluated on off-policy samples.

**Step 1 (TIS, Eq. (2)).** Multiply each token term by $\min(\rho_{i,t},C)$.

**Step 2 (MIS, Eq. (3)).** Replace with a single sequence weight $\rho_i\cdot\mathbb{I}\{\rho_i\le C\}$.

**Step 3 (ACRL, Eq. (10)).** Keep TIS's structure but replace $\rho_{i,t}$ with $\rho_{i,t}^{\alpha}$, $\alpha$ adaptive and signed.

**Result.** ACRL is TIS with an exponent-tempered weight. Setting $\alpha=1$ recovers TIS; $\alpha=0$ recovers vanilla GRPO with no correction at all.

**Correctness check on the bias claims.** The exact correction for a trajectory-level functional is
$$\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mu}\Big[\textstyle\prod_{s=1}^{|a|}\rho_{i,s}\; g(a)\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[g(a)].$$
For a term depending on the prefix $a_{<t}$, the correct weight is $\prod_{s\le t}\rho_{i,s}$, not $\rho_{i,t}$. So the paper is right that TIS is biased (the prefix mismatch is uncorrected), but its characterization of MIS as *"an unbiased gradient estimator"* is inaccurate: masking breaks unbiasedness,
$$\mathbb{E}_\mu\big[\rho\,\mathbb{I}\{\rho\le C\}\,g\big]=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\mathbb{I}\{\rho\le C\}\,g\big]\neq\mathbb{E}_\pi[g],$$
with the discarded mass $\Pr_\pi(\rho>C)$ exactly quantifying the bias. MIS is unbiased only in the untruncated limit $C\to\infty$.

**Missing derivation — why MIS weights vanish.** The paper asserts that $\rho_i$ produces "vanishingly small importance weights" but never derives it. The standard argument: $\log\rho_i=\sum_{t}\log\rho_{i,t}$, a sum of $|a_i|$ approximately independent per-token log-errors with mean $b$ and variance $\sigma^2$, so $\log\rho_i\approx\mathcal{N}(|a_i|b,\,|a_i|\sigma^2)$. Since $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho_i]=1$ forces $b\approx-\sigma^2/2$, the *median* weight decays as $e^{-|a_i|\sigma^2/2}$ while the mean stays at 1, and the effective sample size behaves like $\mathrm{ESS}/n\approx e^{-|a_i|\sigma^2}$. Weight mass concentrates on exponentially few sequences, and with masking almost everything is discarded. This exponential-in-length collapse is the real motivation for ACRL's tempering, and it is absent from the paper.

### 2.2 The constrained-optimization motivation (Eqs. (4)–(7))

**Claim.** Because $\Theta_{E_1}\subseteq\Theta_{E_2}$ for $E_1<E_2$, an over-tight discrepancy bound may exclude $\theta^*$, so accuracy can fall.

**Verification.** The set inclusion is correct and immediate (nested sublevel sets), and hence
$$\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{E_1}}\mathcal{J}(\theta)\;\le\;\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{E_2}}\mathcal{J}(\theta)\;\le\;\max_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta).$$
Correct, but nearly vacuous — it is the monotonicity of a constrained maximum.

**Where the argument fails.** The conclusion drawn ("therefore actively *increase* $E$ when it is small") does not follow. The inequality says that *imposing* a tight constraint can cost accuracy. It says nothing about the case ACRL actually acts on: $E$ being *naturally* small with no constraint active. If no constraint is imposed, nothing is excluded and there is nothing to recover. ACRL's $Y<X$ branch does not relax a constraint; it adds an *anti*-regularizer that pushes $\pi$ away from $\mu$. That is a different intervention with a different justification, and the justification is not supplied.

Two further formal problems: $E=f(Q,\theta,\eta)$ contains a random error $\eta$, so $E$ is a random variable and $\Theta_{E_0}=\{\theta:E\le E_0\}$ is a random set — the constraint should be stated on $\mathbb{E}_\eta[f]$ or $\sup_\eta f$. And the claim *"we can only update the model parameters $\theta$ to systematically adjust the discrepancy $E$"* is questionable, because $E$ passes through a quantizer: as a function of $\theta$ it is piecewise constant within quantization bins and discontinuous across them, so it admits no useful gradient path. Nothing in the constrained program (6) is ever solved — no Lagrangian, no penalty, no dual update. Section 3.1 is motivational rhetoric, not a derivation of the algorithm.

For $Y>X$ we have $1-Y/X<0$, hence $\alpha<0$ when $A>0$ and $\alpha>0$ when $A<0$:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | $>0$ | $\pi<\mu$ | $<0$ | $>1$ | reward ↑ | ✔ |
| II | $>0$ | $\pi>\mu$ | $<0$ | $<1$ | reward ↓ | ✔ |

The $Y<X$ table flips all four, also consistent. The magnitude claim is correct: $|\alpha|=\gamma|X-Y|/X$ is linear in the setpoint error. So the algebra of Eqs. (11)–(12) faithfully implements Figures 3 and 5. This is the one place the paper is fully rigorous.

**Two structural observations the paper misses.** (i) $\alpha$ is *asymmetric*: since $Y\ge 0$, the down-correction branch is unbounded ($Y\to\infty\Rightarrow\alpha\to-\infty$) whereas the up-correction branch is capped at $|\alpha|\le\gamma$. Truncation $\min(\rho,C)$ caps only the upside; $\rho\to 0$ is unfloored, so tokens can have their gradient extinguished entirely. (ii) At $Y=X$, $\alpha=0$ and $\rho_{i,t}\equiv 1$: **at its own setpoint ACRL applies no off-policy correction whatsoever.** Its bias, $\mathbb{E}_\pi[g]-\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho^\alpha g]$, is *maximal* at the design point, equal to the entire uncorrected mismatch $\mathbb{E}_\pi[g]-\mathbb{E}_\mu[g]$. ACRL does not correct the discrepancy; it regulates *deviations of the discrepancy from its initial level*. This is a coherent design, but it is a different objective from what "importance sampling fix" framing suggests.

### 2.4 Reconstruction: what the exponent actually does

The paper offers no analysis of the operator it introduces. Consider the idealized case $\theta=\theta_{old}$ (so $r_{i,t}=1$, no clipping), a single token treated as a bandit with free (tabular) parameterization, and uniform advantage $A\equiv 1$ to isolate the weighting from the reward signal. The ACRL direction is
$$g_\alpha=\sum_a \mu(a)\Big(\tfrac{\pi(a)}{\mu(a)}\Big)^{\alpha}\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a)=\sum_a \mu(a)^{1-\alpha}\pi(a)^{\alpha-1}\nabla_\theta\pi(a)=\nabla_\theta F_\alpha(\pi),$$
$$F_\alpha(\pi)\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_a \mu(a)^{1-\alpha}\pi(a)^{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{H_\alpha(\pi,\mu)}{\alpha},$$
where $H_\alpha$ is the Hellinger integral / Chernoff coefficient, related to Rényi divergence by $H_\alpha=e^{(\alpha-1)D_\alpha(\pi\|\mu)}$. Using $H_\alpha\le 1$ for $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $H_\alpha\ge 1$ for $\alpha<0$ or $\alpha>1$, each with equality iff $\pi=\mu$:

- $\alpha<0$: $F_\alpha$ is **maximized** at $\pi=\mu$ ⇒ the update **attracts** $\pi$ toward $\mu$. For $\alpha=-1$, $F_{-1}=-\big(1+D_{\chi^2}(\mu\|\pi)\big)$, so the update is exactly $\chi^2$-divergence descent.
- $\alpha\to 0$: $F_\alpha\to\sum_a\mu\log\pi+\text{const}$, i.e. $-\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi)$ — plain forward-KL distillation. **Attractive.**
- $\alpha\in(0,1)$: Bhattacharyya-type affinity, maximized at $\pi=\mu$. **Attractive.**
- $\alpha=1$: $F_1=\sum_a\pi=1$, constant ⇒ $g_1=0$. The unbiased IS weight is the **neutral point** (as it must be: $\mathbb{E}_\pi[\nabla\log\pi]=0$).
- $\alpha>1$: $F_\alpha$ is **minimized** at $\pi=\mu$ ⇒ ascent **repels** $\pi$ from $\mu$.

Two consequences that matter for the paper's central claim:

1. **$\alpha=1$ is the attraction/repulsion boundary.** ACRL's exponent is, in this view, a dial on the Amari $\alpha$-divergence family, and $\gamma$ sets how far from the unbiased point the dial can travel.
2. **With $\gamma<1$, ACRL can never repel.** For $A>0$, $\alpha=\gamma(1-Y/X)\le\gamma<1$, so the positive-advantage branch is attractive for *every* value of $Y$, including $Y\ll X$. The reported runs use $\gamma\in\{0.35,0.65\}$. Therefore the mechanism advertised in Figure 4 — "the training probability should be increased further away from the inference probability" — is **not realized** in the experiments. The deeper reason is structural: $\rho^\alpha>0$ always, so a positive multiplicative reweighting can rescale a token's update but can never reverse its direction. The $Y<X$ branch changes only the *relative rate* at which different tokens contract toward $\mu$; it does not create a repulsive fixed point. This is consistent with the paper calling that branch "secondary" (Remark 4.3) and declining to derive it.

A second reconstruction explains the likely real benefit. Weights $\rho^\alpha$ with $|\alpha|\ll 1$ are *tempered/flattened* importance weights; the variance ordering is
$$\mathrm{Var}_\mu[\rho^\alpha]\;\ll\;\mathrm{Var}_\mu[\rho]\;\ll\;\mathrm{Var}_\mu\Big[\textstyle\prod_t\rho_t\Big],$$
i.e. ACRL $\prec$ TIS $\prec$ MIS. Since typically $|\alpha|=\gamma|1-Y/X|\ll 1$, ACRL sits very close to the zero-variance-inflation end. The bias–variance dial, not the "control" narrative, is the most plausible mechanism behind ACRL beating MIS. The paper never performs a variance analysis.

### 2.5 The exploration argument (Eqs. (13)–(14), §4.3)

**Structure of the argument.** (a) Cited prior work: PA&LP and NA&HP tokens raise entropy; PA&HP and NA&LP lower it. (b) Eqs. (13)–(14): tokens with $\pi>\mu$ stochastically dominate tokens with $\pi<\mu$ in absolute probability. (c) Therefore relabel $\pi>\mu$ as "relative high-probability", and read Figure 7 off the quadrant table.

**Problems.** Eq. (14) is the logical complement of Eq. (13) ($\Pr(\pi\le P_0\mid\cdot)=1-\Pr(\pi>P_0\mid\cdot)$), so it is the same statement written twice, not additional evidence. Both are asserted "for all $P_0\in(0,1)$" on the basis of a single density plot from one 3B model — an empirical observation, so *"This mathematically confirms"* overstates it. Strict inequality also cannot hold uniformly at the boundaries. Most importantly, step (c) substitutes a *distributional* fact (dominance between two groups) for the *per-token* property the entropy result requires. A token with $\pi=0.4<\mu=0.5$ is not an absolutely-low-probability token.

**What a rigorous version would look like.** Note $\rho^\alpha$ is constant in $\theta$ (evaluated at $\theta_{old}$), so ACRL is exactly GRPO with a reshaped advantage
$$A^{\text{eff}}_{i,t}=A_{i,t}\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{\mu}\Big)^{\gamma\,\mathrm{sign}(A_{i,t})(1-Y/X)},\qquad \mathrm{sign}(A^{\text{eff}})=\mathrm{sign}(A).$$
For $Y>X$ this gives $|A^{\text{eff}}|=|A|\,(\mu/\pi)^{|\alpha|}$ when $A>0$ and $|A|\,(\pi/\mu)^{|\alpha|}$ when $A<0$. Combining with the standard first-order entropy result for softmax policies, $\Delta H\approx-\eta\,\mathrm{Cov}_{a\sim\pi}\!\big(\log\pi(a),\,A^{\text{eff}}(a)\big)$, the reweighting reduces that covariance provided $\pi/\mu$ is positively associated with $\pi$ — which is precisely what Eq. (13) supplies. Read as a two-group (binary) modulation, this chain is defensible: Eq. (13) yields $\mathbb{E}[\log\pi\mid\pi>\mu]>\mathbb{E}[\log\pi\mid\pi<\mu]$, enough to sign the covariance shift. The continuous-magnitude version is not established. The paper presents neither the entropy formula nor the covariance link, so the mechanism remains a plausibility argument matched to Figure 8.

## 4. Mathematical Foundations

**Borrowed.** GRPO objective and group-normalized advantage (Shao et al., 2024); PPO clipping (Schulman et al., 2017); truncated importance sampling, essentially Ionides-style weight truncation, as applied by Yao et al. (ii) The signed adaptive exponent $\alpha=\gamma\,\mathrm{sign}(A)(1-Y/X)$, which couples a group-level measurement to token-level weights through a power transform. (iii) The four-quadrant AGPA bookkeeping.

**Unclaimed connections that would strengthen the work.** Power-transformed importance weights (tempering/flattening) and their bias–variance behavior; the Amari $\alpha$-divergence / Rényi identification derived in §2.4; and control-theoretic framing — ACRL is a proportional controller in the log-weight domain, $\log\rho_{i,t}=\gamma\,e\,\mathrm{sign}(A)\log(\pi/\mu)$ with error $e=1-Y/X$, whose gain is state-dependent, making the loop bilinear rather than LTI.

## 5. Review

### Theoretical strengths

- The core reframing — regulate the discrepancy toward a reference instead of driving it to zero — is genuinely novel and well-motivated by the observed failure modes of both alignment-based and IS-based fixes.
- The sign/quadrant algebra is internally consistent; I verified all eight cases.
- $\alpha=0$ at the setpoint means the method degenerates gracefully to vanilla GRPO rather than to an unstable regime.
- Remark 4.2 is unusually candid about biasedness, and the diagnosis of MIS's vanishing weights is correct even if not derived.
- The reference $X$ is measured at initialization, which does give a measurement uncorrupted by the training loop's own feedback — a sound instinct.

### Theoretical limitations

1. **The $Y<X$ branch cannot do what it claims.** Because $\rho^\alpha>0$ always, and because $\alpha=\gamma(1-Y/X)<1$ for $A>0$ whenever $\gamma<1$, the mechanism remains attractive toward $\mu$ for every $Y$ (§2.4). Figure 4's stated principle is unrealized at the reported $\gamma$ values. The paper's *"we leave its detailed derivation to the reader"* conceals this.
2. **Sensor–actuator mismatch.** The controller measures absolute distance $d=|\pi-\mu|$, but the actuator's strength is $|\log\rho_{i,t}|=|\alpha|\cdot|\log(\pi/\mu)|$, which depends on the *relative* difference. The paper's claim that "tokens with a large $d$ receive a stronger adjustment" is false: $(\pi,\mu)=(0.9,0.8)$ has $d=0.1$ and $|\log\rho|\approx0.12$, while $(0.002,0.001)$ has $d=0.001$ and $|\log\rho|\approx0.69$. Mean absolute distance is dominated by high-probability tokens; IS weights are dominated by low-probability tokens. These are different quantities.
3. **Bias is maximal at the design point.** At $Y=X$, $\rho\equiv1$ and no correction is applied at all (§2.3). ACRL should not be presented as an alternative *correction* to TIS/MIS; it is a discrepancy-drift regulator.
4. **No closed-loop analysis.** No proof that the plant map $\alpha\mapsto Y_{\text{next}}$ is monotone, no fixed-point existence, no stability or convergence result, no Lyapunov argument. As pure P-control with one-rollout delay and a state-dependent gain, steady-state offset and oscillation at high $\gamma$ are both expected and unaddressed — plausibly what the $\gamma=0.35$ vs $\gamma=0.65$ gap reflects.
5. **No controllability argument for $E$ through the quantizer.** $E$ depends on $\theta$ via a quantizer, hence is piecewise constant/discontinuous in $\theta$. The premise that $\theta$ updates can "systematically adjust $E$" needs support, especially since the discrepancy that matters is partly runtime nondeterminism ($\eta$), which no policy update can influence.
6. **Motivation and algorithm are disconnected.** The constrained program (6) is never solved, penalized, or dualized; nothing in Eqs. (10)–(12) descends from it.
7. **Formal slips.** MIS called "unbiased" despite masking; $E$ used simultaneously as random variable and threshold in Eq. (7); Eq. (14) is a restatement of Eq. (13) presented as an independent fact; "mathematically confirms" applied to a density plot.
8. **Notational inconsistency in $Y$.** The text says "current sequence's discrepancy" and "sequence-level discrepancy $Y$", but Eq. (12) averages over all $G$ responses — $Y$ is *group*-level. Then $\alpha$ is constant across the entire group, and the "sequence-level measurement → token-level ratio" story is really "group-level measurement → token-level ratio", with all token variation entering through $\pi/\mu$.
9. **No variance analysis**, even though variance is the stated failure mode of MIS and the most likely explanation for ACRL's advantage.
10. **Stale setpoint.** $X$ is fixed at initialization while $\theta$, entropy, and response length all drift. And $d$ is entangled with policy sharpness (probability perturbation from a logit error scales roughly as $\pi(1-\pi)\Delta$), so $Y$ is not a clean measure of discrepancy independent of the entropy that ACRL claims to raise. The controller's sensor is coupled to its own side effect, and that loop is unanalyzed.
11. **Non-smoothness at $A=0$.** $\mathrm{sign}(A)$ makes $\alpha$ jump by $2\gamma(1-Y/X)$ across zero advantage, so the weight jumps by a factor $(\pi/\mu)^{2\gamma(1-Y/X)}$. The gradient magnitude is small there, so the practical effect is likely minor, but the objective is discontinuous in the reward.
12. **Unfloored downside.** $\min(\rho,C)$ caps amplification but nothing prevents $\rho\to0$, which silently zeroes gradient contributions from precisely the tokens with the largest relative discrepancy.

### Questions for further investigation

1. Does the $Y<X$ branch ever activate meaningfully, and does the ablation in §5.5 separate "true repulsion" from "differential contraction rate"? Given the $\alpha<1$ argument, the ablation's gains may come from step-size modulation, not from raising the discrepancy. A direct test: run with $\alpha=\max(0,\gamma(1-Y/X))$ clipped to the down-correction branch only.
2. Does $\gamma>1$ (which enables genuine repulsion for $A>0$ at small $Y$) help or destabilize? This is the sharpest test of the paper's stated principle.
3. Is $Y$ actually driven toward $X$ during training, and with what steady-state offset? Reporting $Y_t/X$ trajectories would validate or refute the control claim directly. Would an integral term remove the offset?
4. Would a relative metric — $|\log(\pi/\mu)|$, per-token KL, or reverse-KL — remove the sensor–actuator mismatch of limitation 2? Remark 4.1 defers this, but it is central rather than peripheral.
5. How does ACRL compare against the trivial ablation $\alpha=$ const (tempered TIS, e.g. $\rho^{0.5}$)? If fixed-exponent tempering matches ACRL, the adaptive controller is not doing the work, and the variance explanation of §2.4 is the whole story.
6. How does the setpoint interact with sequence length? Since $Y$ is a per-token mean, longer responses average over more tokens and have lower variance; per-sequence $Y/X$ noise should scale like $1/\sqrt{|a_i|}$, injecting length-dependent control noise.
7. Can the entropy claim be established rather than argued? The covariance formulation in §2.5 is testable: measure $\mathrm{Cov}_\pi(\log\pi,A^{\text{eff}})$ against $\mathrm{Cov}_\pi(\log\pi,A)$ per step.
8. Does the method transfer to the non-quantization discrepancies the introduction promises (kernel mismatch, speculative decoding)? Those change the *support* and correlation structure of $\pi/\mu$, not just its scale, and the fixed initial $X$ may not be a meaningful setpoint there.
9. On the empirical side: AIME24/25 have 30 problems each, so one problem is $3.33\%$. The spread between BF16 ($35.11\%$), TIS ($34.57\%$), MIS ($34.93\%$), and ACRL ($34.65\%$ / $36.39\%$) is within noise, and the two $\gamma$ settings differ by more than any method-to-method gap. The theory's central claim — accuracy gains from controlled discrepancy — is not separable from $\gamma$ tuning at this sample size.

## 总结（中文）

ACRL 的核心思想很有价值：把训练—推理差异当作需要**调节到参考值**的受控量，而不是必须消除的误差。这一视角比"对齐精度"或"无偏 IS 修正"两条既有路线更有工程弹性。算法实现也自洽——我逐一验证了八个象限的符号推导，Eq. (11)–(12) 确实忠实实现了图 3 和图 5 的规则。

## 2. Key Derivations

### D1. Successor representation $\Rightarrow$ value function (Eq. 5 $\to$ 6)

**Start.** $Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t \gamma^t R(s_t)\big]$, $M^\pi(s,a,s')=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t\gamma^t\mathbb{I}(s_t=s')\big]$.

**Steps.** Write $R(s_t)=\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(s_t=s')R(s')$ (valid for finite $\mathcal{S}$), exchange the finite sum with expectation and the $t$-sum (Fubini; justified for bounded $R$ and $\gamma<1$):
$$Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\gamma^t\sum_{s'}\mathbb{I}(s_t=s')R(s')\Big]=\sum_{s'}M^\pi(s,a,s')R(s').$$

**Result / interpretation.** Value factorizes into a policy-and-dynamics term ($M$) and a preference term ($R$). Correct as stated.

**Two unstated restrictions.** (i) Eq. (6) requires reward to depend on state only, which contradicts Eq. (4)'s $R(s_t,a_t)$; §4.2 later repairs this by moving to $(s,a)$ features, but the sum in Eq. (6) should then range over $\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}$. (ii) The experimental state space is **continuous** ($s_t=(\alpha x_t,\alpha y_t)\in\mathbb{R}^2$), where $\mathbb{I}(s_t=s')$ is almost surely $0$ and Eq. (5)–(6) are ill-posed. The correct generalization is either an occupancy *density* or, as actually used, expected discounted feature counts (Eq. 8). The paper never states this transition; Eq. (8) is presented as an approximation of Eq. (6) when it is in fact the only well-defined object in their setting.

### D2. Linear reward decoding $\Rightarrow$ successor features (Eq. 7 $\to$ 8)

**Start.** $R(s)=\phi(s)\cdot w_r$ (assumed exact).

**Steps.** Linearity of expectation over a fixed $w_r$:
$$Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\gamma^t\phi(s_t)^\top w_r\Big]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\gamma^t\phi(s_t)\Big]}_{M^\pi(s,a)}\cdot\,w_r.$$

**Result.** Exact if Eq. (7) is exact; otherwise an $O\!\big(\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}\big)$ approximation (quantified in §3, Lemma A below).

**Critique of the WLOG claim.** The paper asserts Eq. (7) is "without loss of generality, since any component of $\phi(s)$ can learn to replicate the reward $r(s)$." As an *existence* statement this is true and trivial ($d=1$, $\phi_1=R$, $w_r=1$). It does not survive contact with the actual construction for three reasons:

1. **Joint feasibility is not addressed.** The same $\phi$ must simultaneously linearly decode $R$, linearly decode all $J$ costs, and support reconstruction of $(s,a)$ through $g$ (Eq. 14). Each term individually admits a solution; the intersection under a fixed $d$ is a nontrivial constrained representation problem with no analysis.
2. **The normalization $\|\phi\|_2^2=1$ is a genuine restriction, not a convenience.** Restricting $\phi$ to $S^{d-1}$ makes the representable predictor class $\{\langle w,\phi\rangle:\|\phi\|_2=1\}$, bounded pointwise by $\|w\|_2$, and forces all magnitude information into *direction*. The trivial WLOG construction ($\phi_1=R$) is not admissible under this constraint. The paper motivates the normalization purely practically ("consistent input range") without noting that it changes the function class over which Eq. (7)/(12) must hold.
3. **The claim needed for the paper's contribution is much stronger and is never made.** For adaptation, what must hold is that a *new, unseen* cost $C'$ is linearly decodable from the *already-frozen* $\phi$. Nothing in the training objective enforces that. The only mechanism pushing in that direction is the reconstruction term, and an autoencoder yields at best a *nonlinearly* decodable sufficient statistic of $(s,a)$ — linear decodability of arbitrary nonlinear functions of $(s,a)$ from a $d$-dimensional embedding does not follow.

### D3. Cost decomposition (Eq. 12 $\to$ 13)

$K^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\gamma^t C(s_t,a_t)]=w_c\,\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\gamma^t\phi(s_t,a_t)]=w_c M^\pi(s,a)$. Mechanically identical to D2 and correct. Its significance is structural: $Q$ and $K$ share one $M$, so the cost-augmented value is
$$\tilde Q^\pi(s,a)=(w_r-\lambda^\top w_c)\,M^\pi(s,a),$$
i.e. the multiplier acts as a *linear re-weighting in a fixed $d$-dimensional space*, not as a change to the learning target. This is the paper's genuine and elegant core observation, and it is the correct reason SafeDSR should adapt faster than SafeDQN, whose target $\tilde R = R-\lambda C$ shifts under every $\lambda$ update.

**Unaddressed sign issue.** Costs in Eq. (1) are nonnegative; $w_c\phi$ is an unconstrained linear form and can output negative values, so $\hat K$ may be negative and Eq. (15) can award a *cost bonus*. Enforcing nonnegativity (e.g. softplus head) would break the linearity that Eq. (13) requires. This is a real tension in the design with no discussion; whether it bites in practice depends on the unavailable §B/§F diagnostics.

### D4. Vector-valued Bellman residual for $M$ (Eq. 10, 11)

The underlying fixed point is correct:
$$M^\pi(s,a)=\phi(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s',a'\sim\pi}\big[M^\pi(s',a')\big],$$
a $d$-dimensional analogue of the Bellman equation, and a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ componentwise, so the fixed point exists and is unique for fixed $\pi$ and fixed $\phi$.

**The "$\approx$" in Eq. (10) is doing unacknowledged work.** The paper writes
$$\big(R+\gamma Q_{prev}-Q\big)^2\;\approx\;\big\|\phi+\gamma M_{prev}-M\big\|_2^2 .$$
These are not equivalent objectives. The left side is the scalar projection of the right through $w_r$: zero vector residual $\Rightarrow$ zero scalar residual, but not conversely. Minimizing the $d$-dimensional residual is **strictly stronger** and induces a different gradient geometry (it distributes capacity uniformly across feature coordinates rather than along $w_r$). This is arguably beneficial — it is what makes $M$ reusable for *any* re-weighting, including $w_c$ — but presenting it as an approximate identity misstates the relationship. The honest statement is: the vector residual is the objective whose solution is re-weighting-invariant, and the scalar Q-loss is its $w_r$-projection.

**Unspecified target policy — a substantive specification gap.** For Eq. (10) the paper says $a'$ is "the best action selected using the main network." For Eq. (11) (SafeDSR) the selection rule for $a'$ is **not stated anywhere in the provided text**, and the two possibilities have different semantics:

- $a'=\arg\max_a \hat Q$ — then $M$ estimates the SR of the *reward-greedy* policy, and $K=w_cM$ is an off-policy cost estimate for a policy the agent does not execute; the constraint is evaluated for the wrong policy.
- $a'=\arg\max_a(\hat Q-\lambda \hat K)$ — then $M$ tracks the $\lambda$-greedy policy, so $M^\pi$ is itself $\lambda$-dependent and the claimed decoupling of dynamics from the multiplier is only partial (see L2).

Given the argmax in Figure 2 uses $Q-\lambda K$, the second reading is more likely, but this cannot be confirmed without §A. This ambiguity determines whether the method's central claim is exactly or only approximately true, so it should be resolved explicitly in the paper.

### D5. Lagrangian relaxation and the multiplier update (Eq. 3, 15, 16)

**Eq. (3)** is written $\max_\pi\min_{\lambda\ge0}$ (the primal order). What the algorithm implements is $\min_\lambda\max_\pi$ (dual order): the inner problem is solved by value learning, the outer by Eq. (16). Equality of the two requires the zero-duality-gap result, which the paper cites (Paternain et al., 2019) — but cites in support of the wrong statement, claiming the *approach* "is guaranteed to converge to the optimal policy in the limit of infinite data." Paternain et al. establish strong duality (zero gap) for CMDPs over the space of *stochastic* policies under a Slater condition; they do not certify convergence of a particular primal-dual algorithm, and certainly not one with nonlinear function approximation, a single timescale, and a fixed-step multiplier controller.

**Eq. (15) contains a redundant term.** $\arg\max_a\big[Q^\pi(s,a)-\lambda\cdot(K^\pi(s,a)-b)\big]$: the $\lambda\cdot b$ term is constant in $a$ and cannot affect the argmax. Eq. (15) is exactly $\arg\max_a[Q-\lambda K]$. Harmless numerically, but it obscures the real role of $b$, which enters *only* through the multiplier dynamics.

**Eq. (16) has a discount mismatch and a control-theoretic mislabel.**

The exact dual (super)gradient of Eq. (3) with respect to $\lambda$ is $-\big(\mathbb{E}_{p^\pi}[\sum_t\gamma^tC_t]-b\big)$, so projected dual ascent reads
$$\lambda\leftarrow\max\Big(0,\;\lambda+\alpha_\lambda\big(\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_t\gamma^tC_t]-b\big)\Big).$$
Eq. (16) substitutes $C_{ep}$, the realized *episode* cost. Replacing the expectation by one sample is fine (unbiased stochastic ascent). Replacing the *discounted* sum by the *undiscounted* one is not — and the paper's own definitions confirm the undiscounted reading: the cost is "1 for each step taken inside a cost cell," and Eq. (17) tests $C_j(\tau_i)\le b_j$ on raw observed costs. So:

- the decision rule (Eq. 15) compares a **discounted** quantity $K=\mathbb{E}[\sum\gamma^tC_t]$ against the budget scale,
- the multiplier responds to an **undiscounted** violation $\sum_t C_t - b$,
- and the evaluation metric (Eq. 17) is undiscounted.

Three different constraint functionals are in play, related by a $t$-dependent factor. With $\gamma=0.99$ and episodes up to 1000 steps, $\sum_t C_t$ and $\sum_t\gamma^tC_t$ can differ by nearly an order of magnitude for late-episode costs. The paper's assertion that Eq. (16) "is equivalent to optimizing Equation (3) via gradient descent" is therefore false as written; it is dual ascent on a *different* CMDP (the undiscounted-cost one). This is fixable — either discount $C_{ep}$ or define the constraint undiscounted throughout — but it should be stated, since it silently changes which policies are feasible.

Separately, the paper calls Eq. (16) a "proportional controller" and then concludes from the experiments that it "might be improved by incorporating integral and derivative terms." In the control-theoretic framing of Stooke et al. (2020) — the very work being compared against — Eq. (16) *is the integral term*: $\lambda$ accumulates the running constraint violation, $\lambda_t \approx \alpha_\lambda\sum_{k\le t}(C_{ep,k}-b)$. What is missing is the **proportional and derivative** action. The label is inverted, and this matters for the diagnosis: pure integral control on a plant with substantial delay (the policy takes many episodes to respond to a multiplier change) is exactly the configuration that produces overshoot and limit cycles, which is a principled explanation for the oscillatory undershoot the authors observe (SafeDSR/SafeDQN plateau near 70% safe goal rate while the PID variants exceed 80%). The empirical finding is consistent with control theory once the terms are named correctly.

### D6. The adaptation argument (implicit; asserted, not derived)

The paper's contribution claim reduces to: with $\phi$ frozen and $M$ fixed, adapting to a new cost $C'$ requires only solving the supervised regression $\min_{w_c'}\mathbb{E}\big[\|C'(s,a)-w_c'\phi(s,a)\|^2\big]$, after which $\hat K'=w_c'M$ and Eq. (15) yields a new policy.

**No derivation, bound, or convergence statement is given for this.** Specifically absent: (i) how regression error in $w_c'$ propagates to constraint violation; (ii) whether $C'$ lies in $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_i\}$ or how far outside it may lie; (iii) that $M$ was learned as $M^{\pi_{old}}$ while the re-weighted greedy policy is $\pi_{new}\ne\pi_{old}$, so $w_c'M^{\pi_{old}}$ is an off-policy estimate of $K^{\pi_{new}}$. Items (i) and (iii) are the two places where a safety claim could actually fail, and both are quantifiable. I sketch them in §3.

## 3. Theorem Analysis

The paper states no theorems. Below I analyze the borrowed results it relies on, then state the two results that the paper's argument requires but does not provide.

### T1 (borrowed). Zero duality gap for CMDPs — Paternain et al. (2019)

**Statement (informal).** For a CMDP with bounded rewards/costs over the space of stochastic policies, under a Slater condition (some policy is strictly feasible), the primal and dual optima coincide despite non-convexity in the policy parameters.

**Intuition.** Although the parameterized policy optimization is non-convex, the set of achievable (reward, cost) occupancy pairs is convex — mixing two policies realizes any convex combination of their value vectors. Convexity of the *value set*, not of the parameterization, is what strong duality needs.

**How it is used here.** To justify the Lagrangian formulation (§3.1). **How it is over-used:** three gaps between T1 and SafeDSR.

1. **Policy class.** T1's optimum is generally attained by a *stochastic* policy — canonically a mixture of two deterministic policies straddling the budget. SafeDSR's Eq. (15) is a deterministic argmax (plus $\varepsilon$-greedy noise, which is not a *tuned* mixture). For a fixed $\lambda$ the greedy policy is optimal for the surrogate, but at the critical $\lambda^\star$ the surrogate typically has two maximizers whose required mixture cannot be expressed by a deterministic rule. Consequence: the primal-dual iterates chatter between an over-safe and an under-safe deterministic policy rather than converging. This is a structural, not numerical, limitation of value-based Lagrangian CRL and is a plausible second explanation (alongside the pure-integral controller) for the plateau below 100% safe goal rate.
2. **Function approximation.** T1 is stated for exact value/occupancy representations; nothing transfers to $\phi,M$ as neural networks with frozen features.
3. **Algorithm vs. existence.** T1 says the dual optimum equals the primal optimum; it does not say that the specific single-timescale, fixed-step update in Eq. (16) reaches it. RCPO (Tessler et al., 2019), which the paper cites, gets convergence only via a two-timescale argument ($\alpha_\lambda \ll$ critic rate) — a condition SafeDSR neither adopts nor discusses.

### T2 (borrowed, and notably *not* invoked). GPI / SF transfer bound — Barreto et al. (2017)

**Statement (informal).** With successor features $\{\psi^{\pi_i}\}$ for policies optimal under rewards $r_i$, the policy greedy w.r.t. $\max_i \psi^{\pi_i\top}w'$ satisfies
$$Q^{\pi'}_{r'}(s,a)\;\ge\;\max_i Q^{\pi_i}_{r'}(s,a)-\frac{2}{1-\gamma}\min_i\|r'-r_i\|_\infty .$$

**Significance for this paper.** This is the closest existing formal guarantee for "re-weight features, get a good policy," and SafeDSR is architecturally a constrained instance of it. The paper cites Barreto et al. only for the $\phi$-freezing trick, never for the transfer bound.

**Why importing it does not suffice.** The bound is a *lower* bound on the return of the transferred policy under the new scalar reward. Safety needs an *upper* bound on $K^{\pi'}$. Applying the bound to the augmented reward $\tilde r'=r-\lambda^\top c'$ certifies only that $\tilde Q^{\pi'}$ is not much worse than the best base policy — which a policy can satisfy by collecting more reward *and* more cost. **A GPI-style guarantee for the scalarized objective does not imply a constraint-satisfaction guarantee.** Deriving the constrained analogue is, in my reading, the most valuable open theoretical problem this paper exposes, and it is worth stating explicitly as future work rather than leaving implicit.

### Lemma A (missing from the paper; supplied here). Misspecification error is amplified by $\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}$

**Statement.** Fix $\pi$ and $\phi$. Suppose the new cost satisfies $C'(s,a)=w_c'^\top\phi(s,a)+\epsilon(s,a)$ with $\|\epsilon\|_\infty\le\epsilon_\infty$. Then for all $(s,a)$,
$$\big|\,w_c'^\top M^\pi(s,a)-K^{\pi}_{C'}(s,a)\,\big|\;\le\;\frac{\epsilon_\infty}{1-\gamma}.$$

**Proof.** $w_c'^\top M^\pi - K^\pi_{C'}=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t\gamma^t\big(w_c'^\top\phi(s_t,a_t)-C'(s_t,a_t)\big)\big]=-\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t\gamma^t\epsilon(s_t,a_t)\big]$; bound termwise and sum the geometric series. $\square$ (The identical argument with $w_r,\;\delta_\infty$ bounds the $Q$ error from Eq. 7.)

**Why this is not a pedantic remark.** With $\gamma=0.99$, the amplification factor is $100$, against a budget of $b=5$. A per-step cost-regression error of only $0.05$ — 5% of the unit per-step cost in this environment — is enough to consume the *entire* budget in the estimate $\hat K$. Since $w_c'$ is fit by least squares (an $L^2$, not $L^\infty$, criterion) on a *reweighted* sample distribution, $\|\epsilon\|_\infty$ is precisely the quantity least controlled by the training procedure. The paper's fast-adaptation claim therefore rests on an error metric it does not optimize, amplified by a factor of 100. Reporting $\|\epsilon\|_\infty$ (or the realized $|K-K_{\text{MC}}|$ after adaptation) would substantially strengthen §5.4.

### Lemma B (missing; sketch). Off-policy reuse of $M$ after re-weighting

$M^{\pi_{old}}$ is the SR of the old policy. After re-weighting, the executed policy is $\pi_{new}=\text{greedy}(w_rM-\lambda w_c'M)$. Writing $K^{\pi_{new}}=K^{\pi_{old}}+\Delta$, standard simulation-lemma reasoning gives $\|\Delta\|_\infty\le\frac{\gamma\,C_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)^2}\max_s\|\pi_{new}(\cdot|s)-\pi_{old}(\cdot|s)\|_1$ — an $O\big((1-\gamma)^{-2}\big)=O(10^4)$ prefactor, i.e. vacuous unless the policy change is very small. Since the *point* of adaptation is a substantial policy change, the honest conclusion is that **$M$ must also be updated**, and the "only $w_c$ changes" claim is a first-order approximation valid for small cost perturbations. The experiments in §5.4 (which I could not inspect — the text is cut off before them) presumably continue training $M$ during adaptation; if so, the contribution is better framed as *warm-starting* rather than closed-form re-weighting, and the paper's framing overstates it.

## 4. Mathematical Foundations

**Frameworks.** (i) CMDPs and constrained MDP duality (Altman, 1999; Paternain et al., 2019). (ii) Temporal-difference learning / stochastic approximation via contraction fixed points (Watkins & Dayan, 1992). (iii) Successor representation and successor features as a linear factorization of discounted occupancy (Dayan, 1993; Kulkarni et al., 2016; Barreto et al., 2017). (iv) Primal-dual saddle-point optimization with projection onto $\mathbb{R}^J_+$, informally reframed as feedback control (Stooke et al., 2020). (v) Robust statistics for evaluation — interquartile mean (Agarwal et al., 2021).

**Borrowed results.** Zero duality gap (T1); DSR architecture and the reward+reconstruction loss, Eq. (9); $\phi$-freezing for input-distribution stationarity (Barreto et al., 2017); DQN target network + replay (Mnih et al., 2015). A parenthetical cite to Hofmann et al. (2008) supports the offline-adaptation aside; its role is not explained in the text and I could not verify the intended claim.

**Novel theoretical contributions.** Modest and honestly scoped — the paper does not claim theorems.

1. **The linear-augmentation observation (the real contribution).** Because $\tilde R=R-\lambda C$ is affine in $(R,C)$ and both are linear in $\phi$, the multiplier acts as a linear re-weighting $w_r-\lambda^\top w_c$ of a *single* shared $M$. This is the correct structural insight: it makes SF a natural fit for Lagrangian CRL, and it explains why SafeDSR does not need to relearn dynamics when $\lambda$ moves, whereas SafeDQN does. It also strictly improves on the Schmidt et al. (2022) design the paper critiques, where separate $Q$ and $K$ networks redundantly relearn shared dynamics.
2. **Extension of the DSR loss to costs** (Eq. 14) and of features to state-*action* pairs via one-hot concatenation. Straightforward but necessary.
3. **Per-distribution inverse-frequency sampling** ($\tfrac{20\%}{J+1}$ from each of the reward and $J$ cost distributions), generalizing Kulkarni's binary reward oversampling. **Unanalyzed bias:** minimizing an *unweighted* squared loss under a *reweighted* sampling law returns the minimizer of a differently weighted $L^2$ projection. Under exact realizability the minimizer is unchanged; under misspecification (the realistic case, cf. Lemma A) it changes which errors are traded off — and it is applied only to $\phi,w_r,w_c$ while $M$ trains on uniform samples, so the two objectives are projections under different measures.
4. **Safe goal count** (Eq. 17) as an evaluation metric.

## 5. Review

### 5.1 Theoretical strengths

- The linear factorization $\tilde Q=(w_r-\lambda^\top w_c)M$ is a clean, correct, and well-motivated fit between SF and Lagrangian CRL. The mechanism is real, not cosmetic.
- Derivations D1–D3 are algebraically sound; the vector Bellman equation for $M$ is a genuine $\gamma$-contraction for fixed $\pi,\phi$.
- Freezing $\phi$ is theoretically well-chosen: it converts $M$'s learning problem into one with a stationary input distribution, and it is the precondition that makes "adapt $w_c$ only" even coherent.
- Sharp controlled comparison: SafeDQN vs. SafeDSR differ *only* in the parameterization of $Q$, isolating the effect of the SF decomposition. This is good experimental design in service of a theoretical claim.
- The metric critique in §5.2 is legitimate — reward/cost Pareto pairs genuinely lack a task-independent scalarization, and Eq. (17) is more outlier-robust than an empirical $\mathbb{E}[C_j(\tau)]$.
- The paper is appropriately modest, calling its results "initial validation."

### 5.2 Theoretical limitations

**L1 — The central claim is unformalized and unbounded.** Fast adaptation via $w_c$ alone requires that a new cost be linearly decodable from a frozen $\phi$. No condition, bound, or diagnostic is given. Lemma A shows the relevant error metric is $\|\epsilon\|_\infty$ amplified by $\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}=100$ against $b=5$ — so a 5% per-step regression error saturates the budget — while the training objective controls only an $L^2$ error under a reweighted measure. The gap between what is optimized and what must be small is unacknowledged.

**L2 — The decoupling is partial, not full.** $M^\pi$ is policy-dependent, and the policy depends on $\lambda$ and on $w_c$ through Eq. (15). Changing the cost changes the greedy policy and hence $M^\pi$ itself. Lemma B suggests the "$w_c$ only" story is a small-perturbation approximation with an $O((1-\gamma)^{-2})$ worst case. §1 and the abstract ("re-weighting the reachability with a new cost function") read as if the decomposition were exact.

**L3 — Discounted/undiscounted inconsistency across Eq. (15), (16), (17).** $K$ is discounted; $C_{ep}$ and Eq. (17) are undiscounted; $b=5$ is compared against both. The stated equivalence between Eq. (16) and gradient descent on Eq. (3) does not hold as written. This changes which policies are certified feasible.

**L4 — Controller mislabeled; the diagnosis inverts.** Eq. (16) accumulates violation into $\lambda$ and is therefore the **integral** term, not proportional. The paper's conclusion that it "might be improved by incorporating integral and derivative terms" should read *proportional and derivative*. Correctly named, pure-I control with actuation delay predicts exactly the observed overshoot/limit-cycling, giving a principled explanation for the PID baselines' higher safe goal rates.

**L5 — Convergence guarantee over-claimed.** Zero duality gap (T1) is an existence result over stochastic policies with exact representations. SafeDSR uses deterministic greedy selection, neural approximation, single-timescale updates, and a fixed multiplier step. At $\lambda^\star$ the CMDP optimum generally requires a mixture of two deterministic policies, which Eq. (15) cannot represent — so chattering near the optimum is structural. No two-timescale condition ($\alpha_\lambda\ll$ critic rate), as used by RCPO, is imposed or discussed.

**L6 — Constraint attribution under $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration.** With $\varepsilon=0.25$ held fixed, $C_{ep}$ in Eq. (16) is generated by the *behavior* policy, so the multiplier enforces feasibility of a 75/25 mixture of the greedy policy and uniform random action — not of the policy whose values $Q,K$ estimate. A quarter of actions being random is a substantial bias, and it is not a small-$\varepsilon$ correction. This also confounds the baseline comparison in the opposite direction from the authors' own hypothesis: they attribute SafeDSR's advantage partly to high $\varepsilon$ aiding exploration, which is plausible, but the same $\varepsilon$ makes the enforced constraint different from (and harder than) the on-policy one. Both effects deserve an ablation.

**L7 — "WLOG" for Eq. (7) is not justified in the constructed setting.** Joint realizability of reward, $J$ costs, and reconstruction under $\|\phi\|_2=1$ at fixed $d$ is an unanalyzed constrained-representation problem, and the trivial construction offered ($\phi_i \equiv R$) violates the normalization. See D2.

**L8 — Reconstruction gives the wrong kind of sufficiency.** The autoencoder term is the only pressure making $\phi$ a general-purpose encoding of $(s,a)$, but it yields *nonlinear* decodability, whereas Eq. (12) needs *linear* decodability of the new cost. The inferential step from "$\phi$ reconstructs $(s,a)$" to "$\phi$ linearly decodes any new cost" is never made and does not hold in general.

**L9 — Eq. (10)'s "$\approx$" is an inequality of strength, not an approximation.** Zero vector residual implies zero scalar residual, not conversely. The vector objective is strictly stronger, which is desirable here but should be argued, not elided.

**L10 — $a'$ in Eq. (11) is unspecified.** Whether the SR target uses $\arg\max\hat Q$ or $\arg\max(\hat Q-\lambda\hat K)$ determines whether $K$ evaluates the executed policy and whether $M$ is $\lambda$-dependent (L2). Not resolvable from the provided pages; likely in the unavailable §A, but it belongs in the main text.

**L11 — Cost head can predict negative costs.** $w_c\phi\in\mathbb{R}$ is unconstrained while true costs are nonnegative, so Eq. (15) may reward cost. Fixing this with a nonnegative head would break the linearity Eq. (13) depends on. The tension is unremarked.

**L12 — Formal mismatch between Eq. (17) and Eq. (1).** Eq. (1) constrains the *expectation* $\mathbb{E}[\sum\gamma^tC]\le b$; Eq. (17) requires *per-trajectory* satisfaction $C_j(\tau_i)\le b_j$. The latter is closer to a chance/CVaR constraint and is strictly stricter: a policy that is feasible in expectation can score poorly on safe goal count. The metric is defensible, but it evaluates a different problem than the one being optimized, and this should be stated. Additionally, being a *cumulative training* count over a fixed step budget, it conflates final safety with sample efficiency and exploration, and it favors algorithms with shorter episodes (more trajectories per 500k steps) — a systematic confound when comparing off-policy $\varepsilon$-greedy methods against on-policy batch methods.

**L13 — Continuous state space vs. tabular SR.** Eq. (5)–(6) are stated with $\mathbb{I}(s_t=s')$ over a discrete $\mathcal{S}$, then applied where $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^2$; the measure-theoretic step to occupancy densities / expected feature counts is silently skipped.

**L14 — Presentation.** Overloaded $M$ (scalar SR vs. $\mathbb{R}^d$ SF); $R$ as both environment reward and predictor; the vacuous $\lambda b$ in Eq. (15); "a single learnable weight matrix" in the abstract when $w_r,w_c$, $\phi$, and $g$ are all learned (only $w_c$ is re-fit on adaptation); $T$ used as both a fixed and an episode-terminal horizon.

### 5.3 Questions for further investigation

1. **Can Lemma A be turned into a usable certificate?** Given a fitted $w_c'$ with validation residual $\hat\epsilon$, can one bound post-adaptation violation and *reject* adaptations whose $\hat\epsilon$ is too large relative to $b(1-\gamma)$? That would convert a heuristic into a safety mechanism.
2. **What is the constrained analogue of Barreto's GPI bound?** Under $\|c'-c\|_\infty\le\eta$ and re-weighting, is there an upper bound on $K^{\pi'}$ — and does it require a conservative multiplier $\lambda\to\lambda+O(\eta/(1-\gamma))$?
3. **Is $\phi$'s span actually cost-transferable?** Direct test: freeze $\phi$, fit $w_c'$ for a family of held-out cost layouts, and report $\|\epsilon\|_\infty$ vs. $d$. This isolates the paper's core claim from confounds in the end-to-end adaptation experiment.
4. **Does §5.4's adaptation continue to train $M$?** If yes, the claim should be reframed as warm-starting, and the ablation "$w_c$ only, $M$ frozen" becomes the decisive experiment. (Unverifiable here — the text is truncated before §5.4.)
5. **How much of the reported advantage is $\varepsilon=0.25$?** Sweep $\varepsilon$ for SafeDSR/SafeDQN and add matched entropy regularization to the on-policy baselines.
6. **Does an actual PID law on Eq. (16) close the safe-goal-rate gap?** Given L4, adding proportional and derivative terms is the direct test of the oscillation hypothesis, and is cheap.
7. **Does discounting $C_{ep}$ in Eq. (16), making Eq. (15)/(16)/(17) mutually consistent, change results?**
8. **Feature dimension $d$ vs. representable cost class.** Since $\{w^\top\phi:\|\phi\|=1\}$ has bounded capacity, what $d$ is needed for a given cost-layout family, and does the reconstruction weight $\beta_g$ trade off transferability against fit? ($\beta$ values are in the unavailable §B.)
9. **Multi-constraint scaling ($J>1$).** All experiments use $J=1$. With $J$ constraints, $w_c\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}$ must share one $\phi$, the sampling scheme splits $\tfrac{20\%}{J+1}$ per distribution (thinning per-constraint data), and the multiplier dynamics couple. Untested.
10. **Does the deterministic-policy limitation (L5) bind empirically?** Measuring $\lambda$'s oscillation amplitude at convergence, or adding an explicit mixture/stochastic head, would show whether the ~70% plateau is controller-induced or policy-class-induced.

## 总结（中文简述）

本文提出 SafeDSR，将 Deep Successor Representation 扩展到受约束强化学习（CMDP），核心思路是：由于代价增广奖励 $\tilde R = R - \lambda C$ 对 $(R, C)$ 是仿射的，而两者又都线性依赖于特征 $\phi$，因此拉格朗日乘子仅相当于对同一个后继特征 $M$ 做线性重加权，即 $\tilde Q = (w_r - \lambda^\top w_c) M$。这一结构性观察是本文真正的贡献，正确且有价值，也解释了为何 SafeDSR 在代价结构变化时比 SafeDQN 适应更快。

主要理论问题有四点。其一，全文没有任何定理或误差界；"只需更新 $w_c$" 这一核心主张既未形式化也未给出条件。按本报告的引理 A，代价回归的 $L^\infty$ 误差会被放大 $1/(1-\gamma)=100$ 倍，而预算仅为 $b=5$，即每步 0.05 的误差就足以耗尽整个预算——但训练目标控制的是重加权分布下的 $L^2$ 误差，与真正需要变小的量并不一致。其二，$M^\pi$ 依赖于策略，而策略依赖于 $\lambda$ 与 $w_c$，因此解耦只是局部近似而非严格成立。其三，式 (15)(16)(17) 在折扣与非折扣代价之间不一致，"式 (16) 等价于对式 (3) 做梯度下降" 的说法不成立。其四，式 (16) 在控制论意义上是积分项而非比例项，作者据此得出的改进方向（"引入积分与微分项"）正好说反了；若正名为纯积分控制，则带延迟系统的超调与极限环恰好能解释所观察到的振荡与 PID 基线的优势。此外，引用零对偶间隙来声称收敛性过强（该结果面向随机策略与精确表示，而本方法用确定性贪心 + 神经近似 + 单时间尺度更新），$\varepsilon=0.25$ 使乘子实际约束的是行为策略而非目标策略，式 (11) 中 $a'$ 的选取规则在正文未给出。

论文自称 "initial validation"，态度恰当；上述问题多为可修正的表述与一致性问题，但 L1、L2 触及核心主张，建议补充误差界与 "$M$ 冻结、仅更新 $w_c$" 的消融实验。
